Large Language Model (LLM) inference serving faces a
fundamental challenge due to the distinct characteristics of its
two phases: compute-intensive pre fill and memory-intensive
decode. Existing scheduling strategies often prioritize one
phase over the other, leading to a difficult tradeoff between
system throughput and request latency. Prefill-prioritizing
schedulers improve throughput but introduce significant latency jitter (generation stalls) by interfering with ongoing
decodes. Conversely, decode-prioritizing schedulers maintain
low latency but underutilize GPU resources, resulting in low
throughput. This paper revisits the technique of chunked prefills, demonstrating its efficacy in mitigating this tradeoff. By
splitting large prefill computations into smaller, manageable
chunks and interleaving them with decode operations using
stall-free batching, we can leverage the compute slack inherent in the decode phase. This approach significantly improves
serving capacity under strict latency constraints, minimizes
generation stalls, and reduces pipeline bubbles in distributed
deployments, enabling efficient and responsive inference.

1 Introduction
The remarkable capabilities of Large Language Models
(LLMs) [13, 14, 18, 20, 27] have fueled their rapid adoption across diverse applications, making LLM inference a
dominant workload in modern data centers [7]. Optimizing
the cost and user experience of LLM serving is paramount.
LLM inference proceeds in two phases: an initial prefill phase
that processes the input prompt in parallel to generate the
first output token, followed by an iterative decode phase that
generates subsequent tokens autoregressively, one at a time.
The prefill phase is compute-bound, efficiently utilizing
GPU resources, while the decode phase is memory-bound, often leaving compute units underutilized, especially at smaller
batch sizes [1 9, 28]. This dichotomy creates a fundamental
throughput-latency tradeoff for LLM serving systems. Schedulers like vLLM [19] prioritize prefills to maximize batch

This paper summarizes the approach presented in SarathiServe [8, 9], which employs chunked prefills and stall-free
hatching to mitigate this tradeoff. Chunked prefills break
down large, potentially stall-inducing prefill operations into
smaller chunks. Stall-free batching intelligently interleaves
these prefill chunks with ongoing decode operations by utilizing the decode phase's inherent compute slack, without
pausing the decode stream. This allows the system to maintain high GPU utilization and throughput while significantly
reducing generation stalls and TBT variance, leading to a
more responsive user experience. We briefly discuss the core
mechanism and highlight its benefits, including how chunking
enables advanced parallelism for further scaling.

Autoregressive Inference Phases

LLM inference is an autoregressive process with two distinct
phases:
Prefill Phase. The input prompt tokens are processed in parallel through the Transformer layers to generate the first output
token. This phase is compute-bound due to the parallel processing and potentially large n2 attention computation.
Decode Phase. Subsequent tokens are generated one by one.
In each step, the previously generated token is fed back into
the model to predict the next token. This requires the attention mechanism to consider all previously generated tokens .
To avoid redundant computations, the K and V vectors for
all processed tokens are stored in a KV cache. Accessing
this growing KV cache makes the decode phase increasingly
memory-bandwidth bound, as only one token is processed per
request per iteration.
The time to generate the first token is the Time-To-FirstToken (TTFT), critical for interactivity. The time between
subsequent tokens is the Time-Between-Tokens (TBT), impacting perceived fluency.

The Challenge: Prefills vs. Decodes

3.1

Contrasting Resource Demands

As outlined above, pre fill computation is typically computebound, while decode computations processing single tokens
is memory-bound. This difference in bottlenecks is central to
the scheduling challenge. The compute slack available during
the memory-bound decode phase presents an optimization
opportunity if it can be utilized without hindering decode
progress.

4.2

Pipeline Bubbles

Stall-Free Batching

Leveraging the compute slack available during decode iterations and the smaller compute units created by chunking,
stall-free batching constructs hybrid batches containing both
ongoing decode tokens and one (or more) prefill chunks from
newly arrived or partially processed requests. Crucially, it
does not pause ongoing decodes to execute prefills. Instead,
it carefully sizes the prefill chunk(s) added to a batch such
that the total compute load fits within a target iteration time
(token budget), determined by the application's TBT SLO.
As shown conceptually in Figure 2 (bottom), prefill chunks
(,CpJ " 'Cp 2', etc.) are coexecuted with decode iterations ('Ad',
'B/) without causing stalls. This ensures that decode tokens
are processed without significant delay, effectively eliminating generation stalls caused by pre fill interference. By coscheduling pre fills and decodes, it maximizes GPU utilization, boosting throughput. Furthermore, the resulting hybrid
batches have more uniform execution times compared to
batches in traditional prefill-prioritizing systems, significantly
reducing pipeline bubbles in PP deployments.

In distributed deployments using Pipeline Parallelism (PP)
[16], the varying execution times of micro-batches (iterations)
containing different mixes of prefill and decode tokens cause
pipeline bubbles (GPU idle time) , reducing overall efficiency
as shown in Figure 3. Prefill-heavy iterations take much longer
than decode-heavy iterations, forcing downstream stages to
wait, causing large pipeline bubbles.

Chunked Prefills

Instead of processing an entire input prompt in a single prefill
iteration, chunked prefills divide the prompt into multiple,
smaller chunks (e.g., 512 or 2048 tokens) . Each chunk is
processed in a separate iteration. This is motivated by two insights. First, Prefill computation often saturates GPU compute
capacity even with moderately sized inputs (e.g., a few hundred tokens). Second, contrary to earlier assumptions, the
overhead of chunking (e.g., repeated KV cache reads for
attention) is often minimal, especially for models employing optimizations like Multi-Query/Grouped-Query Attention (MQA/GQA) [11 , 22] and for the long contexts where
chunking is most needed [10]. Chunking transforms long,

GPU configurations (see Table 1) and two datasets (see Table 2). We consider vLLM and Orca as baseline because they
represent the state-of-the-art for LLM inference. Our evaluation seeks to answer the following questions:

Workloads: In order to emulate the real-world serving scenarios, we generate traces by using the request

ChatGPT-4 [2]. A conversation may contain multiple rounds

prompt lengths. In contrast, arxiv_summarization is a collection of scientific publications and their summaries (abstracts)

and Google Duet AI [4] etc. The request arrival times are generated using Poisson distribution. We filter outliers of these
datasets by removing requests with total length more than
8192 and 16384 tokens, respectively.

Capacity Evaluation

We evaluate Sarathi-Serve, Orca and vLLM on all four models
and both datasets under two different latency configurations:
relaxed and strict. Similar to Patel et al. [21 ], to account for
the intrinsic performance limitations of a model and hardware
pair, we define the SLO on P99 TBT to be equal to 5 x and
25 x the execution time of a decode iteration for a request
(with prefilliength of 4k and 32 batch size) running without
any prefill interference for the strict and relaxed settings,
respectively. Table 3 shows a summary of the absolute SLO
thresholds. Note that the strict SLO represents the latency
target desired for interactive applications like chatbots. On
the other hand, the relaxed configuration is exemplary of
systems where the complete sequence of output tokens should
be generated within a predictable time limit but the TBT
constraints on individual tokens is not very strict. For all load
experiments, we ensure that the maximum load is sustainable,
i.e. , the queuing delay does not blow up (we use a limit of 2
seconds on median scheduling delay).
Figure 5 and Figure 6 show the results of our capacity
experiments. Sarathi-Serve consistently outperforms Orca
and vLLM in all cases across models and workloads. Under
strict SLO, Sarathi-Serve can sustain up to 4.0 x higher load
compared to Orca and 3.7 x higher load than vLLM under
strict SLO (Yi-34B, openchacsharegpt4). For larger models
using pipeline parallelism, Sarathi-Serve achieves gains of up
to 6.3 x and 4.3 x compared to Orca and vLLM respectively
(LLaMA2-70B, openchacsharegpt4) due to few pipeline bubbles.

Conclusion

Optimizing LLM inference for high throughput and low latency is desirable but challenging. We presented a broad characterization of existing LLM inference schedulers by dividing
them into two categories - prefill-prioritizing and decodeprioritizing. In general, we argue that the former category is
better at optimizing throughput whereas the latter is better
at optimizing TBT latency. However, none of them is ideal
when optimizing throughput and latency are both important.
To address this tradeoff, we introduce Sarathi-Servea system that instantiates a novel approach comprised of
chunked-prefills and stall-free hatching. Sarathi-Serve chunks
input prompts into smaller units of work to create stall-free
schedules. This way, Sarathi-Serve can add new requests in a
running batch without pausing ongoing decodes. Our evaluation shows that Sarathi-Serve improves the serving capacity
of Mistral-7B by up to 2.6 x on a single AlOO OPU and up to
5.6 x for Falcon-180B on 8 AlOO OPUs.

Making Pipeline Parallel Viable

We now show that Sarathi-Serve makes it feasible to efficiently serve LLM inference across commodity networks
with efficient pipeline parallelism. For these experiments, we
run Falcon-180B over two nodes, each with four AlOO OPUs,
connected over 100 Obps Ethernet. We evaluate model capacity under three configurations: vLLM with 8-way TP, vLLM
with our pipeline-parallel implementation and Sarathi-Serve
with pipeline-parallel. For PP configurations, we do 4-way
TP within node and 2-way PP across nodes.
