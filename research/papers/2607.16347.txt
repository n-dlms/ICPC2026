1

Moore’s 1968 paper gives two greedy views of these capacities [18]. The familiar Moore–Hodgson
algorithm scans jobs by deadline and deletes a longest job whenever a prefix becomes late. Moore’s
original shortest-processing-time (SPT) procedure instead scans jobs from shortest to longest and
keeps the next job if the retained set remains feasible. The insertion rule never revokes an acceptance,
and only jobs of equal processing time require a choice. Its direct implementation, however, may
test every later deadline prefix for every candidate and therefore takes quadratic time.
Lin and Wang analyze the insertion rule as a dual greedy algorithm and state an O(n log n)
bound, but they do not specify the dynamic representation needed to maintain the feasibility
test within that bound [17, Sec. 3]. An earlier dynamic-programming account of Moore–Hodgson
keeps, for every EDD prefix and every attainable cardinality, one feasible set of minimum total
processing time [22]. Hoogeveen and T’kindt use the direct quadratic insertion test in a related
variable-start-time problem and ask whether it can be improved [13]. Zhao and Yuan subsequently
obtain O(n log n) algorithms for that setting and for the insertion rule itself [23, 24]. Their latter
implementation maintains a preëmptive DD-schedule as occupied intervals separated by idle gaps. A
candidate may cross several interval records, after which the schedule representation is restored; the
logarithmic bound is amortized over the complete scan.
The deadline tree developed here has the same total asymptotic bound, but it stores neither a
schedule nor an interval decomposition. At each accepted deadline it stores one processing mass, and
every subtree stores only two summary fields: total work and least intrinsic slack. These summaries
compose associatively along the deadline order. A candidate follows one root-to-leaf path. A right
turn accumulates work known to precede the candidate’s deadline; a left turn certifies the current
deadline and an entire skipped right subtree. A rejected candidate changes no state. Thus each
decision and each accepted point update takes O(log(q + 2)) worst-case time with q stored deadlines.
Once an SPT order is given, the scan takes O(n log(k + 2)) time, where k is the number of distinct
deadlines in its output; including sorting, it takes O(n log n) time. A proportional family with
α.d = 32 α.p gives a matching Ω(n log n) algebraic computation-tree lower bound. A paired-deadline
family gives the same bound when every processing time is one and an SPT order is supplied, so
the logarithm is not merely the cost of finding the processing-time order. A separate fixed-state
construction shows that, even after arbitrary preprocessing, a single feasibility query on q stored
deadlines can require Ω(log q) time.
The same deadline-prefix capacities also determine the structure of every tie order. Our main
contributions are as follows.
1. We isolate the exact suffix-slack margin for one insertion and derive the deadline tree from
an associative ordered-block summary. We also give a coördinate-compressed segment-tree
specialization and compare the representation with Zhao and Yuan’s DD-schedule data structure.
The resulting implementation has a worst-case logarithmic bound for every candidate. In the
algebraic computation-tree model, a proportional family proves the total O(n log n) bound
optimal, a paired-deadline family preserves that lower bound for unit jobs with a supplied
SPT order, and a fixed-state construction proves the O(log(q + 2)) query bound optimal after
arbitrary preprocessing.
2. For every attainable cardinality i, the first i accepted jobs, over all SPT tie orders, are exactly
the feasible i-sets of minimum total processing time. Conversely, every such set is realized
by a tie order. These sets simultaneously maximize the number of selected jobs below every
processing-time threshold, so their sorted processing-time vectors are componentwise minimum.
3. After the shorter processing-time tiers have been fixed, the feasible choices from the next
2

tier form a canonical nested matroid. The complete greedy outputs are the bases of the
direct sum of these tier matroids, and ordering a tie class by a secondary weight is precisely
the Rado–Edmonds greedy algorithm. Across all cardinalities, the optimal prefixes form the
ordered sum of the tier matroids, a local poset greedoid; every fixed-cardinality layer is the
basis family of an explicit matroid.
4. A compact flow network packages all deadline prefixes into a normalized, monotone, submodular
rank function. Feasibility is the condition that every job contributes its full processing mass.
After contracting the shorter accepted jobs, dividing residual rank by the current processing
time and taking the floor recovers the entire rank function of the corresponding tier matroid.
The common object behind these results is residual deadline-prefix capacity. The insertion margin
records its least value in an affected suffix; the tree maintains that value dynamically; the tier matroids
quantize it within one processing-time class; and the flow construction packages all thresholds into
one polymatroid rank. Earlier work already supplies the insertion rule, final-cardinality lexicographic
optimality, one nested minimum-work list for a fixed tie order, and an O(n log n) implementation
[22, 17, 24]. The scheduling-specific contributions here are the two-number deadline summary, the
exact description of all optimal prefixes and tie orders, the canonical tier capacities, and their
recovery from contracted flow.
All sets are finite, all release times are zero, and processing times and deadlines are positive
real numbers. Running times use the comparison-based RAM model with unit-cost arithmetic and
comparisons on input values; the lower bound is stated separately in the algebraic computation-tree
model.
Organization. Section 2 fixes the notation and proves the insertion criterion. Section 3 states

3.1

Relation to Moore’s original procedure

Moore starts with an SPT order. He finds the first late job, puts the prefix ending there into EDF
order, and keeps the candidate exactly when that reordered prefix is feasible [18, pp. 103–105]. The
deadline-ordered deletion rule attributed to Hodgson is a different algorithm, although both solve
the same problem [21, 10, 5].
Theorem 3.1 (Equivalence with Moore’s SPT algorithm). Fix an order within every equal-processingtime tier. Moore’s SPT algorithm and Algorithm 1 retain the same jobs.
Proof. We induct over Moore’s iterations. At the start of an iteration, let R be the jobs already
retained. The current sequence consists of a feasible EDF order of R, followed by the unexamined
jobs in the fixed SPT order. This invariant holds initially with R = ∅.
In searching the current sequence for its first late job, Moore may pass an initial block P of the
unexamined suffix. For every prefix P ′ of P , the current sequence schedules R ∪ P ′ on time. Hence
R ∪ P ′ is feasible, and Algorithm 1 accepts the jobs of P one by one. If Moore finds no late job,
then P is the entire remaining suffix and both algorithms terminate with the same set.
Otherwise, let α be the first late job. Immediately before α is considered by Algorithm 1, its
accepted set is R ∪ P . Moore now orders R ∪ P ∪ {α} by deadline. By Lemma 2.1, this EDF order is
feasible exactly when R ∪ P ∪ {α} is feasible, which is exactly the condition under which Algorithm 1
accepts α. Thus both procedures make the same decision.
If α is retained, Moore’s reordered prefix is a feasible EDF order of the new retained set. If α
is rejected, the remaining set is R ∪ P , which was already shown feasible; after removing α, the
reordered prefix is an EDF order of that set and is therefore feasible by Lemma 2.1. In either case the
unexamined SPT suffix is untouched, so the invariant is restored. Induction proves the theorem.
The distinction is algorithmic, not merely terminological: the insertion rule never revokes an
accepted job. Moore–Hodgson scans by deadline and may delete a job accepted earlier.

4.1

The deadline tree

Algorithm 1 is stated in terms of feasibility. Conceptually, one may implement one iteration by
tentatively adding the candidate and undoing the addition if the new set is infeasible:
X ← X + α;

if Slack(X) < 0 then X ← X − α.

This formulation is a useful specification, but it changes the state even when the candidate is
rejected. We can instead answer the same question read-only by first evaluating CanAdd(d, p); only
a successful query is followed by a point update.
At every deadline d, the tree stores only the accepted processing mass
mX (d) = Proc(X=d ).
No schedule is stored. The data structure supports CanAdd(d, p), for p ≥ 0, and a signed point
update Add(d, ∆), subject to mX (d) + ∆ ≥ 0. Its state is therefore a nonnegative deadline–mass
profile. When that profile comes from a job set, a client using a negative update must change the
represented job collection consistently. The SPT scan uses only positive updates.

Theorem 4.3 (Fast implementation). For any chosen SPT order σ, the deadline-tree implementation
returns G(σ). Once the order is known, the scan takes O(n log(k + 2)) time, where k is the number
of distinct deadlines in its output. Including the sort, the running time is O(n log n).
Proof. After every iteration, the tree represents exactly the accepted set X, and X is feasible. By
Theorem 4.2, the query for α succeeds exactly when X + α is feasible. Thus the tree makes precisely
the decisions of Algorithm 1.
Let qi be the number of keys present when the ith job is tested. The iteration performs one
O(log(qi + 2)) query and, only on acceptance, one update of the same order. Accepted jobs are never
removed, so qi ≤ k. The scan therefore takes O(n log(k + 2)) time; comparison sorting by processing
time takes O(n log n).
Once the jobs are supplied in SPT order, the scan is online with respect to that stream: no
decision uses a later job. The tree needs neither advance knowledge of the deadline universe nor
coördinate compression. An implementation may repeat the key search after a successful query and
keep the query’s auxiliary space constant, or retain the O(log(k + 2))-length search path and reuse
it for the update.

4.1.5

Algebraic lower bounds

The preceding algorithms take O(n log n) time overall and O(log(q + 2)) time per deadline-tree query.
We use Ben-Or’s standard algebraic computation-tree model, in which arithmetic and root-extraction
nodes form new real quantities from the inputs, previously computed quantities, and real constants,
while branch nodes test signs [1]. The next three results show that both logarithms are unavoidable
in this model. The proportional family gives the shortest overall proof. The paired-deadline family
removes the cost of finding an SPT order. The final construction fixes the stored state in advance
and varies only one candidate.

Overview of the Zhao–Yuan data structure

The purpose of the present section is to highlight the main ideas underlying the Zhao–Yuan data
structure. Our overview is not intended to be comprehensive; see [24] for full details. Our goal is to
allow the reader to appreciate the relative simplicity of the augmented BST data structure presented
in Section 4.1.
At a high level, the Zhao–Yuan data structure is based on three main ideas. The first idea is to
exploit the fact that a job set admits a feasible non-preëmptive schedule if and only if it admits a
feasible preëmptive schedule. In other words, the Zhao–Yuan data structure determines whether
X + α is feasible by determining whether there is a feasible preëmptive schedule for X + α.
The second idea is to observe that, in order to construct a feasible preëmptive schedule for a
given feasible job set, we can schedule each successive job α as late as possible; that is, we allocate
unused intervals working backwards from the deadline of α until the processing time of α has been
fulfilled.
The above two ideas are sufficient to provide the basis for a correct data structure. We can store
the maximal contiguous allocated intervals associated with the current schedule in a BST ordered by
the interval start times. To check whether we can add a job α with deadline d and processing time
p, we first search the BST to identify the rightmost allocated interval with start time at most d. We
then move left through the interval records while maintaining a running sum of the unused time
available before d, until either (1) the sum reaches p, so α can be added while preserving feasibility,
or (2) time zero is reached and the sum remains less than p, so α cannot be added while preserving
feasibility.
A single test may encounter many interval records. If each movement between records is
implemented by an ordered-dictionary operation, the immediate bound is O(k log n), where k is
the number of records encountered before one of the two terminating conditions is reached. When
condition (1) is reached, however, inserting α as late as possible transforms the portion of the
schedule encountered by the search. The restoration procedures consolidate that portion, so all but
a constant number of the records crossed by the search disappear as separate records. Thus the
work spent crossing those records can be charged against the resulting decrease in the size of the
representation. This gives the desired amortized logarithmic bound for successful tests. The same
conclusion does not yet follow for a test ending in condition (2), because a rejected job need not
alter the representation. The third idea removes this difficulty.
The third main idea is to observe that if a job α with deadline d and processing time p cannot be
added to the current feasible set X, then no job β that is processed by the SPT insertion rule after
α and that has deadline at most d will ever be accepted. Indeed, suppose that β were accepted later,
when the accepted set had grown to X ′ ⊇ X. Then β.p ≥ p and β.d ≤ d. Replacing β by α in a
feasible schedule of X ′ + β preserves feasibility, so its subset X + α would be feasible, a contradiction.
We may therefore modify the preëmptive schedule on the prefix relevant to deadline d: rather than
continuing to execute the jobs represented there as late as possible, we schedule them from time
zero as early as possible, in EDF order. The affected interval records can then be consolidated into a
single initial occupied interval. Consequently, a rejected test also pays for the records encountered
by its search, and the same amortized analysis applies to both outcomes.
The invariant produced by these operations is called a DD-schedule. An initial portion of the
accepted work is packed from time zero in EDF order; the remaining work is scheduled strictly after
this initial portion and as late as possible. Equivalently, the first occupied interval begins at time zero,
while each later occupied interval is anchored at a deadline (that is, its right endpoint is a deadline),
with idle gaps separating successive occupied intervals. The ordered family of maximal occupied

14

intervals, and hence the idle gaps determined by their endpoints, is the associated DD-interval family.
The implementation need not retain the full assignment of job pieces to time intervals: it is enough
to maintain this interval family and the information needed to perform the backward search and
restore DD form.
Thus a candidate may cross several records, and either outcome may transform the portion
crossed by the search. Zhao and Yuan bound the total number of ordered-dictionary operations over
the complete scan by a potential argument, obtaining O(log n) amortized time per candidate and
O(n log n) total time; see [24, Lem. 2.4 and Theorem 2.1].
The Zhao–Yuan structure and our augmented BST implement the same accept-if-feasible rule
and have the same overall asymptotic running time. The difference lies in the representation and the
analysis. The Zhao–Yuan structure maintains a preëmptive schedule through occupied intervals and
idle gaps, and its time bound is amortized over the resulting interval transformations. Our structure
maintains no schedule: it stores one processing mass at each accepted deadline and two intrinsic
summary fields in each subtree. Each feasibility query follows one read-only search path; a rejected
candidate therefore causes no state change, and every test has a worst-case logarithmic bound.

Edmonds greedy within a tier

Theorem 6.1 turns the tie-breaking part of SPT into a standard greedy problem. At any point in

ordering finds a basis of a matroid: every maximal independent set is a basis.
More generally, ties can be used to optimize a secondary objective.

A flow-polymatroid view

equal-processing-time tier.

The tree and the flow rank are two resolutions of the same deadline-prefix capacity. The tree
implements the full-marginal decision dynamically; the flow rank packages all thresholds into one
submodular function. The polymatroid does not replace the exchange argument: its greedy algorithm
may allocate only part of a job, and the family of feasible job sets need not be a matroid. Its discrete
role is exact but tierwise. After contraction by the shorter jobs and division by p, its rank becomes
the nested-matroid rank tested during the processing-time-p phase of SPT.

8

Conclusion

Deadline-prefix capacity is the common object behind the algorithmic and structural results. Locally,
its least remaining value in an affected suffix is the exact insertion margin. The deadline tree stores
this quantity in its subtree summaries but exposes only the Boolean decision required by the SPT
scan. A logarithmic root-to-leaf traversal accumulates work on right turns and certifies skipped
suffix blocks on left turns; an accepted candidate then causes one logarithmic point update, while
a rejected candidate leaves the state unchanged. The algebraic lower bounds show that both the
total O(n log n) time and the O(log(q + 2)) query time are sharp in their model; the former remains
sharp for unit jobs with a supplied SPT order.
Within a fixed processing-time tier, the same capacities, measured in units of the tier size and
tightened by suffix minima, become nested cardinality bounds. They define a chain-presented laminar
matroid. Combining the tiers explains every complete greedy output and every minimum-work
acceptance prefix, including the local poset greedoid and the fixed-cardinality layer matroids. In
particular, tie-breaking can optimize an arbitrary additive secondary objective without changing the
primary optimum.
Globally, threshold cuts in the flow network define a scheduling polymatroid rank. Contracting
the shorter accepted jobs and scaling its increments recovers the full rank function of the current
tier matroid. Suffix slack, tier capacities, and flow cuts are therefore three resolutions of the same
nested deadline-prefix constraints.
