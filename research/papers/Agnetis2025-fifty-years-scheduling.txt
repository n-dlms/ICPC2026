Abstract
Abstract This paper presents an overview of scheduling research done over the last half century. The
main focus is on what is typically referred to as machine scheduling. The first section describes the general framework for machine scheduling models and introduces the notation. The second section discusses
the basic deterministic machine scheduling models, including single machine, parallel machines, flow shops,
job shops, and open shops. The third section describes more elaborate models, including multi-objective
and multi-agent scheduling models, scheduling with controllable processing times, scheduling with rejection,
just-in-time scheduling, scheduling with due date assignments, time-dependent scheduling, and scheduling
with batching and setups. The two subsequent sections consider scheduling under uncertainty; section four
goes into online and robust scheduling and section five covers stochastic scheduling models. The next section
describes a variety of important scheduling applications, including applications in manufacturing, in services,
and in information processing. The last section presents the main conclusions and discusses future research
directions.

Introduction
Scheduling is a decision-making process that plays an important role in many different types of organisations.

It deals with the assignment of jobs to resources and the timing of their processing while adhering to given
constraints in such a way that one or more objectives are optimised. Jobs and resources can take many different
forms [Pinedo, 2022]. Resources may be, for example, personnel, machines in a workshop, runways at an airport
or processing units in a computer, while jobs may be customers requiring service, operations in a production
process, takeoffs and landings at an airport, or executions of computer programs. Each job may have one or
more characteristics, such as processing time on each resource, due date, importance (weight), and earliest
possible start time (release date). The scheduler’s objectives may take diverse forms, and the different forms
may go against one another. An objective may be, for example, the minimisation of the completion time of the
last scheduled job (commonly known as the makespan), or the minimisation of the number of jobs completed
after their due dates. A scheduling problem may also be subject to various types of constraints, such as time
constraints, resource capacity constraints, or precedence constraints among the jobs.
One of the first building blocks of scheduling theory was introduced in the beginning of the 20th century
with the Gantt chart of Henry L. Gantt, who used it to compare production schedules. With the emergence
of the Operations Research discipline, the first scheduling papers started to appear after the second world
war, e.g., [Johnson, 1954], [Jackson, 1955], [Smith, 1956] and [Bellman, 1956]. The first Operations Research
oriented journals were launched in the 1950s, namely Management Science, Operations Research, Naval Research
Logistics, Operational Research Quarterly. They served in the very beginning as the main publication outlets
for the scheduling community. This was followed up by a number of other journals, mainly aiming at the
Operations Research, Production Research, Discrete Mathematics and Computer Science disciplines, which
have been the domains for sharing new scheduling ideas and results ever since. One of the players in this field
is the European Journal of Operational Research (EJOR), which was founded in 1977 by the Association of
European Operational Research Societies. In 1998, the Journal of Scheduling (JOS) was founded which is now
considered the flagship journal of the scheduling community.
At the end of the 1960s the first textbook on scheduling, by [Conway et al., 1967] appeared, which established

some of the applications of scheduling theory in practice. Finally, Section 7 presents conclusions and discusses
some future research directions.

Basic notation in deterministic scheduling

In a typical deterministic scheduling problem a set of n jobs has to be scheduled on a set of m machines.
An operation of job j on machine i is referred to as Oij . An instance for such a problem includes various job
parameters, which may be problem-dependent. Job parameters that are commonly used include: (i) processing
time (pij ), which is the time required to process job j on machine i; (ii) release date (rj ), which is the earliest
time that the processing of the first operation of job j can start; (iii) due date (dj ) which is the time at which
the processing of the last operation of job j is due to be completed; and (iv) weight (wj ), which is a priority
factor representing the importance of job j.
It is common to denote a scheduling problem using the standard three-field α|β|γ notation introduced
by [Graham et al., 1979]. The α field describes the machine environment, the β field describes the processing
characteristics and constraints, and the γ field specifies the scheduler’s objective.
The flow of jobs among the machines depends on the specific machine environment. The most basic environment is the single machine (α = 1), where each job has a single operation requiring processing on the
machine. Two types of more complex environments can be described as follows. The first is that of parallel
machines, where each job has a single operation to be processed on one of m machines that work in parallel
and the second is the set of shop environments, where each job has multiple operations and each operation of
a job has to be processed on a specific machine. The most common parallel machine environments are: (i)
identical machines (α = P m), where the job’s processing times are machine independent (pij = pj ); (ii) uniform machines (α = Qm), where machine i operates at speed vi , and pij = pj /vi ; and (iii) unrelated machines
(α = Rm), where the jobs’ processing times are both job- and machine-dependent.
Shop scheduling environments differ from one another in the way the jobs flow through the system. The most
common shop environments are: (i) the flow shop (α = F m), where all jobs are processed by all m machines in
the same order, from machine 1 to machine m in increasing order of machine index; (ii) the job shop (α = Jm),
where each job has its own predefined route to follow through the machines; and (iii) the open shop (α = Om),
where the route that a job has to follow through the machines is decided upon by the scheduler. Note that in
all machine environments the "m" represents a case where the number of machines is constant. However, when
the number of machines is given as a part of the instance, it is omitted from the α field.
There are several machine environments that are hybrids, i.e., they include aspects of shops and parallel
machine environments. For example, in a flexible flow shop environment (F F ), at times also referred to as
either hybrid flow shop or multiprocessor flow shop, the machines are set up in m consecutive stages. The flow
of each job is from stage i to stage i + 1 for i = 1, ..., m − 1. At each stage there is a bank of parallel machines
and the scheduler has the flexibility to decide which machine at stage i processes operation Oij . The F F entry

A scheduling problem may have a set of unique characteristics and constraints, that are included in the β
field of the three-field notation. There are many possible unique characteristics and constraints, including: (i)
precedence constraints (prec), indicating that one or more jobs may have to be completed before another job
is allowed to start its processing (precedence constraints may have specific structures with different impacts on
the problem complexity, see [Prot & Bellenguez-Morineau, 2018]); (ii) release dates (rj ), indicating that job j
cannot start before rj ≥ 0 (otherwise, it is common to assume that rj = 0 for each job j); (iii) preemption
(prmp), when it is possible to interrupt the processing of a job (preempt it) at any time, and complete it later
on without losing the effort that was already invested in it; and (iv) permutation (prmu), which is a restriction
that is common in the flow shop environment, implying the scheduler has to maintain the same job processing
order on each of the m machines. Note that definitions of special cases of a problem may also appear in the β
field, e.g., pij = pj implies that the job processing times are job-dependent but not machine-dependent.
Performance measures (usually denoted by capital letters) enable the scheduler to measure the quality of
a schedule for a particular job. Given a schedule, the most important performance measure for job j is its
completion time, Cj , as all other performance measures are functions of the completion times. Examples of
performance measures common in the scheduling literature are Fj = Cj − rj , which is the flow time of job
j; Lj = Cj − dj , which is the lateness of job j; Tj = max{0, Cj − dj }, which is the tardiness of job j;
Yj = min{pj , Tj }, which is the late work of job j; Ej = max{0, dj − Cj }, which is the earliness of job j; and Uj
which is a binary tardiness indicator that equals 1 if Cj > dj and 0 otherwise.
In order to compare different schedules with one another and choose the best among them, an objective
function that aggregates the performance measures of all jobs into a single function is needed. The objective
function (or scheduling criterion) is specified in the γ field of the three-field notation. There are two main sets

Complexity and solution methods for deterministic models

Scheduling is an interesting field where combinatorial optimisation tools can be applied and tested. Some
of the main tools developed for analysing combinatorial optimisation problems, have early on been applied to
scheduling problems. An important set of concepts and tools used in the analysis of scheduling problems is
provided by the theory of Computational Complexity (see, e.g., [Garey & Johnson, 1979]). It is assumed that
the reader is familiar with the main concepts of this theory, namely the time complexity of a discrete algorithm
and the main problem classes (e.g., P, N P, N P-hard and N P-complete). In most scheduling problems the
size of the input is measured by the number of jobs n (sometimes also by the number of machines m). So, most
often a polynomial problem is one for which a solution algorithm exists that runs in time O(p(n)), where p(n) is
a polynomial function of n, while for an N P-hard problem such an algorithm does not exist (unless P = N P),
i.e., finding the solution may take in the worst case a running time that is exponential in n. Also recall that
a pseudo-polynomial time algorithm is an algorithm that runs in polynomial time with respect to the largest
integer appearing in problem instance and the instance size, n. If a pseudo-polynomial time algorithm exists
for an N P-hard problem, then it is only N P-hard in the ordinary sense. If, however, a problem is strongly
N P-hard, then there does not exist a pseudo-polynomial time algorithm (unless P = N P). Ever since the
development of the computational complexity theory, one of the main goals in the analysis of a combinatorial
optimisation problem is to classify its hardness based on this theory. Two websites, whose main contributors
are Brucker, Dürr, Jäger, Knust, Prot, van Stee and Vásquez, keep an updated list of the main results: https:
//www2.informatik.uni-osnabrueck.de/knust/class/ and http://schedulingzoo.lip6.fr/".
For providing an optimal solution for hard scheduling problems, dynamic programming [Bellman, 1957],
branch-and-bound [Land & Doig, 1960], and integer linear programming (ILP) solution methods [Blazewicz et al., 1991]
have been developed. Until the 1990s, certain scheduling problems, such as Jm||Cmax , were the subject of major
competitions for the best combinatorial branch-and-bound algorithms and researchers developed increasingly sophisticated methods to solve specific instances. ILP techniques have also been proposed for scheduling problems,
but with limited success. The first ILP formulations for solving Jm||Cmax were proposed by [Wagner, 1959],
[Bowman, 1959] and [Manne, 1960]. These formulations form a basis for the main modeling techniques for
scheduling problems, namely models with binary position variables (i.e., variables xjk = 1 iff job j is in position
k), binary time-indexed variables (xjt = 1 iff job j is in process at time t), and binary variables indicating a
linear ordering (xij = 1 iff job i precedes job j in the schedule). These techniques made it possible to solve
scheduling problems using general-purpose ILP solvers. The first solvers developed in the 1980s were VINO,
developed by LINDO systems, and CPLEX, developed by CPLEX Optimisation. This software evolved in parallel with research embedding the most efficient techniques. Today, commercial and open-source solvers, such
as CPLEX, Gurobi, OR Tools, XPRESS, etc. can be used readily with programming languages such as C++,

Tailored mathematical programming models can be used to solve efficiently several scheduling problems to
optimality or to obtain tighter bounds in approximation methods. In this respect, [Queyranne & Schulz, 1994]
present a systematic polyhedral analysis of various formulations for single machine scheduling, including facetdefining inequalities and separation algorithms. Their work paved the way to sophisticated approaches for
deriving strong lower bounds and even LP-based approximation algorithms, e.g., polynomial-time approximation
P
algorithms with performance ratios better than 2 for 1|prec| wj Cj [Schulz, 1996]. However, more progress
is to be expected for solving efficiently more difficult scheduling problems such as, for example, the Jm||Cmax
problem, using ILP solvers [Della Croce, 2016]. In the 1960s, the Artificial Intelligence community introduced
Constraint Programming (CP) to solve constraint satisfaction problems. A constraint satisfaction problem is
defined by variables, domains, and constraints and a solution is an assignment of values to decision variables in
their domains so that the constraints are satisfied. CP brings together a set of techniques aimed at gradually
reducing the size of the set of feasible solutions through a tree search algorithm. These techniques have been
developed in the 1970s and 1980s, with the appearance of "global" constraints, which are constraint expressions
that call on efficient procedures for dealing with certain types of constraints. The first software tools were created
in the 1970s and 1980s (Prolog, Alice, CHIP, etc.). Since then, CP has become a powerful tool for solving a large
category of problems, including scheduling problems, and is competitive for particularly constrained problems
(see, e.g., [Caseau & Laburthe, 1994] and [Artigues & Feillet, 2008]). A wide variety of commercial and opensource CP solvers are available for researchers, e.g., AIMMS, CP Optimiser.
If a problem instance is too hard to solve optimally, an alternative approach is to generate, in a reasonable
time, a very good solution through a heuristic algorithm or simply a heuristic. Among these algorithms,
the simplest are certainly the greedy algorithms. These algorithms build a solution step by step, without
ever questioning any decisions made previously. Examples are the heuristic algorithm by [Nawaz et al., 1983]
for F m|prmu|Cmax and the algorithm by [Chang & Sullivan, 1990] for Jm||Cmax . These algorithms generally
have a relatively low complexity, which makes them fast, but they may return a rather low quality solution.
Metaheuristics are problem-independent, combinatorial problem-solving methodologies that provide strategies
for building heuristics. The term metaheuristic also refers to a specific implementation of an algorithm based
on a certain methodology. Early metaheuristic algorithms include evolutionary algorithms [Holland, 1975]
and [Goldberg, 1989], simulated annealing [Kirkpatrick et al., 1983] and tabu search algorithms [Glover, 1986].
Since these first methods, the panoply of metaheuristics has greatly expanded, with in particular ant colony
optimisation (ACO) [Dorigo et al., 1991] and particle swarm optimisation [Kennedy & Eberhart, 1995]. These
methodologies have given rise to efficient heuristics for many scheduling problems, and they serve more and
more as a reference, being both fast and providing good quality solutions.

Approximation algorithms are algorithms with a performance guarantee. A ρ-approximation algorithm (for
a minimisation problem) returns solutions whose quality does not exceed ρ times the value of the optimal
solution (ρ > 1). The parameter ρ is called the relative performance guarantee. Approximation schemes are
algorithms that take an ε parameter as input and generate a solution with a value that does not exceed (1 + ε)
times the value of the optimal solution; ε being fixed, a polynomial time approximation scheme (PTAS) has
polynomial complexity with respect to the size of the instance n but is non-polynomial with respect to 1/ε. A
fully polynomial time approximation scheme (FPTAS) is polynomial with respect to both n and 1/ε. A detailed
presentation of approximation algorithms can be found in [Williamson & Shmoys, 2011]. In complexity theory,
the set of APX problems includes all N P optimisation problems that allow polynomial time approximation
algorithms with approximation ratio bounded by a constant. Unless P = N P, it can be shown that there are
problems (called APX -hard problems) that are in APX but do not admit a PTAS; that is, problems that can be
approximated within some constant factor, but not any constant factor. Therefore, PTAS reductions are used
for providing inapproximability results (which obviously hold only if P =
̸ N P). The first works that propose
approximability results date back to the early 1970s and even today, the approximability of some problems
remain open (see, e.g., [Schuurman & Woeginger, 1999], [Hoogeveen et al., 2003] and [Naor et al., 2007]).
A new category of heuristic algorithms has been receiving more and more attention by researchers. In this category, a solution neighborhood is defined, as in other metaheuristic algorithms, but each solution’s neighborhood
is then searched optimally using mathematical programming techniques [Maniezzo et al., 2021]. Such methods,
referred to as matheuristics, have been applied to several scheduling problems (see, e.g., [Tarhan & Oğuz, 2022]).
Similarly, CP-heuristic methods have been introduced, where the exploration of the neighborhood is done using CP techniques. A commercial software called Hexaly [Benoist et al., 2011] offers a modeling language and
resolution techniques based on integer programming, CP, and metaheuristic algorithms. This software has been
used with success in solving difficult scheduling problems [Blaise et al., 2020].

1.3.

Uncertainty in scheduling

In deterministic scheduling it is assumed that all the data concerning a problem, e.g., processing times,
release dates, due dates, etc., are fixed and known in advance. Therefore, at time zero, minimising the objective
function gives rise to a deterministic combinatorial optimisation problem. However, uncertainty is a common
phenomenon in scheduling applications.
Two uncertainty paradigms that have received attention in the literature are the robust scheduling paradigm
and the online scheduling paradigm. In robust scheduling, there are two different approaches to present an
instance. The first is via the multi-scenario approach, where a set of discrete scenarios is given, and the values
of some job parameters are scenario-dependent. The second is via the interval approach where the value each

scheduling, in which only the distribution function of each uncertain parameter is known in advance. The actual
realisations of the random variables only become known during the evolution of the scheduling process.

2.1.

Single machine scheduling

The simplest scheduling problems deal with scheduling single-operation jobs on a single machine to minimise a regular scheduling criterion. In such cases, there exists an optimal solution with no unforced machine idle time, so a solution is simply defined by a job processing sequence. The two best-known results
for single machine problems are two monotone sequencing rules. The Earliest Due Date (EDD) rule prescribes a sequence of jobs in non-decreasing order of their due dates and this sequence is optimal for 1||Lmax
[Jackson, 1955]. The Weighted Shortest Processing Time (WSPT) rule sequences the jobs in non-decreasing
P
order of pj /wj , which is optimal for 1|| wj Cj [Smith, 1956]. Note that the former is an example of a
max-type objective, whereas the latter is an example of a sum-type objective (see Section 1.1).

2.2.

Parallel machine scheduling

Consider now scheduling problems with parallel machines. Here jobs still consist of a single operation,
but the problem is twofold: each job has to be assigned to one of m machines, and a job schedule must be
found for each machine. Compared to single machine problems, parallel machine problems typically turn out
to be harder due to the fact that partitioning and scheduling decisions now have to be integrated. Consider,
for example, the case where the objective is to minimise the makespan. In the single machine case, any job
permutation is optimal as scheduling decisions become redundant. Nevertheless, partitioning decisions make
problems with more than a single machine harder. In fact, P 2||Cmax is N P-hard in the ordinary sense, due to
its equivalence to partition, while the decision version of P ||Cmax is equivalent to the decision version of the
classical bin-packing problem, and both problems are strongly N P-hard.
For parallel machine scheduling problems, a very common and intuitive approach is list scheduling (LS),
which has been thoroughly analysed over the years and is also commonly used for online problems (see Subsection 4.1). It consists of ordering the jobs in a list, and then assigning the next job on the list to the
machine that frees up first. LS algorithms differ from each other based on the ordering rule applied to the jobs
on the list. A fundamental work by [Graham, 1969] established that LS provides an approximation ratio of
4/3 − 1/(3m) for P m||Cmax if the jobs are ordered according to the longest processing time first (LPT) rule
(i.e., in a non-increasing order of processing times). A slight variant of LPT, based on pre-assigning a suitable
set of jobs to one machine, yields a bound of 9/8 for m = 2 and a 4/3 − 1/(3(m − 1)) bound for m ≥ 3
[Della Croce & Scatamacchia, 2020]. Still, the best known worst-case of a constant factor approximation algorithm for P 2||Cmax (excluding approximation schemes) is 12/11, achieved with a linear-time (but non-LS) algorithm [He et al., 2000]. easy [McNaughton, 1959], even with uniform machines [Gonzalez & Sahni, 1978, Lawler & Labetoulle, 1978]
and additional resources [Slowinski, 1978].

2.3.

Flow shop scheduling

By far the most studied flow shop problem is F m||Cmax . It is assumed (if not explicitly stated) that
a sufficiently large buffer exists between each pair of successive machines, so that jobs can be resequenced
between any one machine and the next. If resequencing is forbidden, a solution is completely defined by a
job permutation, and the problem is referred to as a permutation flow shop (denoted as F m|prmu|Cmax ). A
well-known basic result is that, in problem F m||Cmax , there is no advantage in any resequencing between M1
and M2 , or between Mm−1 and Mm ; implying that, as long as m ≤ 3, an optimal solution for F m|prmu|Cmax
is also optimal for F m||Cmax .
Curiously enough, perhaps the oldest published scheduling result does not concern a single machine problem, but rather a two-machine flow shop. This is of course Johnson’s well-known algorithm for F 2||Cmax
[Johnson, 1954], which is a nice example of how an intuitive idea (precedence should be given to jobs that
quickly provide work for the second machine, while jobs requiring a short time on the second machine should
be done at the end) can be formally translated into an exact and efficient algorithm (requiring O(n log n) time).
For m ≥ 3, both F m||Cmax and F m|prmu|Cmax are strongly N P-hard [Garey et al., 1976].
One of the most widely studied special cases of the flow shop is the so-called ordered flow shop, which is
common in many industries. This special case is defined by two properties. The first is that if for two jobs j and
k the inequality p1j < p1k holds, then pij ≤ pik for all i ∈ {2, ..., m}. The second is that if for two machines i
and i′ , the inequality pi1 < pi′ 1 holds then pij ≤ pi′ j for all j ∈ {2, . . . , n}. [Smith et al., 1976] proved that there
exists an optimal V-shaped solution for F m|prmu, ordered|Cmax , where jobs are ordered first according to SPT
followed by jobs ordered according to LPT (note that there exists an exponential number of such schedules).
However, the optimal schedule for the less restricted F m|ordered|Cmax problem is not necessarily a permutation
schedule. [Smith, 1968] proved that the F 3|ordered|Cmax is easy when the largest processing times occur either
on the first or on the last machine, while if this is not the case the F 3|ordered|Cmax problem becomes N P-hard
in the ordinary sense [Hou & Hoogeveen, 2003]. The proportionate flow shop is even a more restricted special
case of the ordered flow shop with the processing times being machine-independent, that is pij = pj . For this
case, any permutation is optimal for the corresponding F m|pij = pj |Cmax problem. In fact many (but not all)
proportionate flow shop problems have an optimal solution that is identical to its corresponding single machine
P
counterpart. e.g., the SPT rule is optimal for the F m|pij = pj | Cj problem and the EDD rule is optimal for
P
F m|pij = pj |Lmax . In contrast, the WSPT rule is not optimal for F m|pij = pj | wj Cj , although it is so for

Consider now the case where the intermediate buffers between machines have a finite capacity c. It implies
that when the buffer between machine i and machine i + 1 is full (i.e., it contains c jobs) a job completed by
machine i remains on i, keeping it blocked, until room is freed up in the buffer. In fact, even for m = 2, the
problem becomes strongly N P-hard for any 1 ≤ c ≤ n − 2 [Papadimitriou & Kanellakis, 1980]. However, this
is not the case when c = 0, i.e., no buffer exists between the stages. This situation gives rise to two different
shop variants, namely blocking (jobs can wait on a machine, keeping it blocked), and no-wait (a job is allowed
to spend on the machine only the time strictly necessary for its processing). When m = 2, the two variants are
equivalent. The nice feature of the two-machine no-wait flow shop problem is that it is a special case of the
TSP, in which the cost of scheduling j immediately after k is represented by the idle time on machine 1, which
is max{0, p2k − p1j }. A TSP with this structure is equivalent to a sequencing problem on a one-dimensional
state machine (see Section 2.1), in which the state of the system after processing k is represented by p2k and it
has to be brought to p1j in order to process j. Hence, this problem can be solved in O(n log n) time. For m ≥ 3,
F m|no − wait|Cmax is still a special case of TSP, but it is strongly N P-hard [Röck, 1984]. Several scheduling
problems with blocking and no-wait constraints are reviewed by [Hall et al., 1996, Allahverdi, 2016].
Over the years, a number of papers have appeared proposing heuristic and metaheuristic approaches for
F m||Cmax and F m|prmu|Cmax . One of the most successful heuristic approaches for F m|prmu|Cmax is the
greedy Nawaz-Enscore-Ham (NEH) heuristic [Nawaz et al., 1983]. This heuristic prescribes ordering the jobs
according to LPT (with respect to the total processing time), and inserting the k-th job in the best position
among the first k − 1 jobs, for k = 2, . . . , n. Despite its simplicity, even after 40 years, NEH is still a reference
in constructive heuristics for flow shops. With regard to metaheuristics, the best tradeoff between computation
time and solution quality is probably achieved by the Iterated Greedy (IG) procedure by [Ruiz & Stützle, 2007].
In its most basic version, it consists of iteratively discarding a certain subsequence of jobs from a solution and
re-constructing the solution by rearranging the jobs following the NEH greedy heuristic.
In terms of approximation algorithms, the 5/3-approximation algorithm for F 3||Cmax by [Chen et al., 1996]
is still the best available. On the other hand, there is no polynomial time ρ-approximation algorithm for
F 3||Cmax with ρ < 5/4 [Williamson et al., 1997]. Regarding permutation flow shops with an arbitrary number
of machines, an efficient branch-and-bound algorithm for F |prmu|Cmax by [Gmys et al., 2020], with a new node
decomposition scheme, a learning mechanism and a parallel implementation, was shown to solve very large
instances of the problem to optimality. A survey of 50 years of research on flow shop scheduling appears in
[Gupta & Stafford, 2006].
While the flow shop model has been studied extensively, significant attention has also focused on flexible
flow shops, which are often encountered in practice. In their review, [Ruiz & Vazquez-Rodríguez, 2010] reported

2.4.

Job shop scheduling

Job shop scheduling problems are prevalent in many industries. The literature about job shop scheduling
contains a wide variety of problems, with many different characteristics (operators, transportation resources,
auxiliary tools, etc.) and constraints (release dates, no-wait, setup times, batches, energy consumption, machine
availability, etc.). The job shop problem was first tackled in the 1950s using mathematical programming
techniques [Bowman, 1959, Wagner, 1959, Manne, 1960]. The problem most often studied is Jm||Cmax . Its
special case with two machines and without recirculation (i.e., each job visits each machine at most once) can be
solved in polynomial time, through an algorithm that extends Johnson’s algorithm for F 2||Cmax [Jackson, 1956].
However, J2||Cmax with recirculation is strongly N P-hard [Lawler et al., 1977].
Even with very few jobs, the job shop problem is challenging. [Brucker, 1988] devised the currently most
efficient algorithm for the job shop problem with two jobs (J|n = 2|Cmax ), having complexity O(h log h), where
h is the number of operation pairs belonging to the two different jobs and requiring the same machine. With
three jobs, even problems J3|n = 3|Cmax and F |n = 3|Cmax are N P-hard, as are many other job shop problems
with a fixed number of jobs [Sotskov & Shakhlevich, 1995, Brucker & Sotskov, 2007].
Many (exact or heuristic) approaches to the job shop problem are based on a graph-theoretic representation
of the problem called disjunctive graph [Balas, 1969]. The nodes correspond to tasks and the arcs express
precedence constraints either among the tasks of the same job or among the tasks requiring the same machine,
the latter being a result of scheduling decisions. In fact, J||Cmax can be stated as the problem of deciding the
orientation of disjunctive arcs so that the longest path on the graph (i.e., the makespan) is minimised.
Exact algorithms for J||Cmax have been developed for many years. The competition between branch-andbound algorithms was severe in the 1980s and 1990s. The challenge was to solve benchmark instances in the

Probably the most famous heuristic algorithm for J||Cmax is the Shifting Bottleneck algorithm by [Adams et al., 1988].

2.5.

Open shop scheduling

While job shop scheduling can be viewed as a generalisation of flow shop scheduling, open shop problems
display a significantly different structure. As in the previous shop models, each job j must be processed on each
one of m machines, requiring a processing time pij on machine i. Unlike in previous cases, the m operations of
each job can now be carried out in any order, i.e., there are no precedence constraints among operations. In a
classical open shop, a job cannot be processed by two machines at the same time. Despite the fact that open
shops have been studied in depth over the years (as pointed out by [Strusevich, 2022], the very first formulation
of the problem is presented as an exercise in a classical textbook by [Bellman, 1957], under a different name),
new extensions are still being proposed regularly.
The simplest open shop problem is O2||Cmax , for which many different linear-time algorithms have been proposed over the years [Gonzalez & Sahni, 1976, Pinedo & Schrage, 1982, Khramova & Chernykh, 2021]. While
O3||Cmax is N P-hard in the ordinary sense [Gonzalez & Sahni, 1976], the complexity of Om||Cmax with a fixed
m ≥ 4 is still open. However, when m is not fixed (i.e., O||Cmax ), it is strongly N P-hard even to establish
whether there exists a schedule with a makespan not larger than 4 [Williamson et al., 1997]. With regard to approximation algorithms, [Sevastianov & Woeginger, 1998] provided a PTAS for Om||Cmax . [Sevastianov, 1995]
used a result known as the Compact Vector Summation Theorem to characterise polynomial special cases of
Om||Cmax (in particular, when the ratio of a machine’s load to the longest processing time is sufficiently large).
The preemptive open shop problem O|prmp|Cmax has strong connections with other areas of combinatorial
optimisation and algebra. Given an instance of O|prmp|Cmax , one can build a bipartite multigraph in which the
nodes on the two sides correspond to jobs and machines respectively, and there are pij edges joining the nodes
corresponding to machine i and job j. A matching on such a multigraph corresponds to a unit time interval
during which no two machines are active on the same job. Hence, the minimum makespan equals the minimum
number of disjoint matchings covering all the edges of the graph, which can be found in polynomial time (see
e.g., [Cole et al., 2001]). The equivalence between preemptive open shop schedules and graph-theoretic concepts
is exploited in some problem extensions, e.g., including release and due dates [Sedeno-Noda et al., 2009], or additional renewable or non-renewable resources [Slowinski, 1979, Cochand et al., 1989, Jurisch & Kubiak, 1997].

Recently, two reviews addressed makespan minimisation in open shops. [Strusevich, 2022] reviewed many
different algorithms for O||Cmax , discussing details regarding different implementations and the underlying
theory. This review focuses on complexity and approximation issues. In turn, [Ahmadian et al., 2021] analysed
the literature classifying various contributions and cover exact and heuristic algorithms as well as CP approaches
for N P-hard open shop problems.
While the literature on makespan minimisation in open shops is huge, other objectives have received less
attention, because for most objectives the problems (either preemptive or non-preemptive) are N P-hard even
when m = 2. A very broad and complete view on open shop scheduling is provided in the book by [Kubiak, 2022],
which besides reviewing classical results focuses also on several problem variants and extensions with objectives
other than makespan. These variants include, among others, concurrent open shops (in which a job can be
processed on more than one machine at the same time), multiprocessor open shops (in which an operation can
be carried out by any machine of a given subset), and no-wait open shops. It is worth noticing that while
F 2|no − wait|Cmax is polynomially solvable, O2|no − wait|Cmax is strongly N P-hard [Sahni & Cho, 1979] (as
well as O2|prmp, no − wait|Cmax , as shown in [Kubiak, 2022]).
With regard to flexible open shop problems, [Lawler et al., 1982] showed that in the preemptive case the
problem is solvable by decomposing it into several instances of P |prmp|Cmax and then a single instance of
O|prmp|Cmax , which can all be solved very efficiently. Of course, the non-preemptive problem is hard, and
various results exist concerning approximations, exact algorithms and metaheuristics, which are reviewed by
[Adak et al., 2020], along with relevant applications. A large number of open problems concerning open shops
can be found in [Kubiak, 2022].

3.3.

Scheduling with controllable processing times

In classical deterministic scheduling, the jobs’ processing times are assumed to be fixed (see Section 1.1).
However, in practice a scheduler can control processing times by allocating additional resources (in the form of
fuel or manpower) to the processing of an operation. In such cases, the scheduler can take advantage of the
flexibility in job processing times to improve scheduling performance. [Janiak, 1987] described an application
of a scheduling problem with controllable processing times in steel mills, where batches of ingots have to be
preheated before being hot-rolled in a blooming mill, and both the preheating time and the rolling time are
inversely proportional to the gas flow intensity. Other relevant applications include scheduling in a machine
tooling environment (see, e.g., [Kayan & Akturk, 2005] and [Trick, 1994]), and supply chain scheduling with
outsourcing options [Shakhlevich & Strusevich, 2006].

3.5.

Just-in-time scheduling and due windows scheduling

In many scheduling problems a due date is given for each job and one wants to minimise some tardiness
measure. However, in various situations, the scheduler does not want the jobs to be completed too soon. For
instance, if a product cannot be delivered to the customer before a certain due date, completing it early only
builds up inventory and hence entails a cost. In such problems, the due date represents the ideal date at
which a job should be completed, and both positive and negative deviations from this due date are penalised.
This is the class of just-in-time scheduling problems, which has gained attention with the adaption of modern
operations management approaches approaches such as just-in-time (JIT) production and lean production.
These approaches have emphasised the importance of eliminating (or at least, reducing) in-process inventory
as opposed to simply maximising productivity. According to [Baker & Scudder, 1990] the JIT concept can be
translated into a scheduling objective in several ways, with the most common objective being the minimisation
of the total (weighted or not) earliness and tardiness. An alternative objective, also widely used, is to maximise
the weighted number of jobs that are completed in JIT mode (i.e., completed exactly at the due date).

In the JIT-scheduling literature, another major topic refers to due windows, which is a generalisation of due
dates [Janiak et al., 2015]. A job is considered JIT if it completes within a given time window, which may be
either common to all jobs or job-specific. In a common window scenario, maximising the number of JIT jobs
is still polynomial, but when due windows are job-specific most problems are N P-hard, e.g., maximising the
number of JIT jobs on a single machine. Many such results are reviewed in the book by [Jozefowska, 2007].

3.6.

Scheduling with due date assignment

In classical scheduling models the main assumption is that job due dates are predetermined. With the goal
of improving the efficiency of systems, the main objective in scheduling with due date assignment (DDA) is to
provide managers with tools to optimally coordinate DDA with scheduling decisions. Such integrated models
improve managers’ ability to determine attainable due dates that take into account the ability of the production
system to meet them. In a second research direction in scheduling with DDA, the due dates are given in advance,
but not their assignment to jobs, which has to be determined by the decision maker.
Consider first the case where job due dates are decision variables. There are several methods to determine
the due dates, which differ mainly by the types of restrictions on their values. The three most popular DDA
methods are: (i) the CON method, where all jobs have to be assigned the same due date, that is dj = d for
j = 1, ..., n, and d ≥ 0 is a decision variable; (ii) the SLK method, where dj = pj + slk for j = 1, ..., n, and
slk ≥ 0 is a decision variable; and (iii) the DIF method, where there are no restrictions with regard to the due
date assigned to each job.

Time-dependent scheduling

While one of the main assumptions in most scheduling problems is that processing times are given (and most
of the times deterministic), time-dependent scheduling problems deal with situations in which the processing
time of job j depends on its start time t, i.e., pj = pj (t). In some cases jobs deteriorate, i.e., their processing
times increase with time. This may be the case with fire fighting tasks and with maintenance tasks. In other
cases, job durations may shorten with time, such as the detection time of an approaching object.
Research has addressed scheduling problems with various objective functions (mostly classical) and different
forms of pj (t) functions. While in principle the function pj (t) may be arbitrary, until the 2000s the only cases
studied in the literature were linear time-dependent functions, either increasing or decreasing. In such cases
certain properties hold. For example, for the single machine case, when pj (t) = bj (a + bt) the makespan
does not depend on the job sequence, while it does in the slightly more general case pj (t) = aj + bj t (aj
and bj being job-dependent parameters). Nevertheless, 1|pj (t) = aj + bj t|Cmax is solvable in O(n log n) time

3.8.

Batching and setups

Since the 1990s, an increasing amount of research has focused on scheduling problems with batching. Such
models deal with situations in which jobs must be aggregated into lots or batches. Jobs in the same batch are
processed together, which brings about two types of batching environments, namely serial and parallel batching.
In serial batching (s-batching), jobs of the same batch are processed one after the other, and a setup time
occurs only at the start of each batch. A further distinction within s-batching models can be made with regard
to the way jobs are released by the machine, referred to as job availability versus batch availability. In job
availability models, each processed job is available upon its own completion, so it maintains its individual
completion time, as in classical scheduling. For instance, individual mechanical parts in the same batch are
consecutively processed, but each part is available as soon as it is completed. In batch availability models, all
the jobs of a batch become available upon completion of the last job of the batch; so all the jobs of the same
batch have the same completion time. For instance, all parts in a batch are mounted on a pallet and only when
all parts are machined the whole pallet is released. Also, several papers refer to family setups, i.e., jobs are
partitioned into F families and a setup occurs when the next job belongs to a different family (e.g., for cleaning
or refixturing). In this scenario, each batch contains jobs of the same family.
In parallel batching (p-batching) models, all jobs in a batch are processed and released together, and the
processing time of a batch is the processing time of the longest job in the batch. This is the case with burn-in
operations in circuit board manufacturing, which are performed in ovens that can accommodate several jobs at
the same time. In both s-batching and p-batching, the batch size can either be bounded by b or not.

Another broad category of scheduling models that has been developed over the years, especially over the last
two decades, concerns scheduling problems with setups. In these problems, setups may occur between jobs and
have to be taken into account when minimising time-related objectives. Besides the usual classification based
on system structures and objective functions, in his extensive review [Allahverdi, 2015] categorises the problems
on the basis of setup types, which may or may not have a family structure, and can be sequence-dependent
(the setup time between jobs i and j depend on both i and j), sequence-independent (may depending only
on j), or past-sequence-dependent when it is proportional to the total processing time of the jobs preceding j
(which casts the problem close to a time-dependent scheduling problem). As the simplest scheduling problem
with sequence-dependent setups is a TSP, it is not surprising that most scheduling problems with setups are
hard, and a large majority of the 500 papers reviewed in [Allahverdi, 2015] contain heuristic or metaheuristic
approaches.

Although both robust and online scheduling models assume some level of uncertainty regarding the job
parameters, the analytical tools are quite similar to those of deterministic scheduling. This is in contrast to
stochastic scheduling that requires a different type of analysis. The next two subsections focus on the online
and robust scheduling paradigms. Stochastic scheduling models are considered in the section that follows.

4.1.

Online Scheduling - the basics

Online scheduling models differ from offline scheduling models in the following aspects: (i) In offline models,
all job parameters are known in advance, whereas in online models the information about the job parameters
is gradually released to the decision maker. (ii) In offline models, the scheduler can schedule all the jobs in
advance, while in online models the scheduling decisions have to be made during the evolution of the process.
(iii) In both offline and online models, a certain objective function has to be minimised. However, in online
models, the lack of knowledge about the future usually prevents the scheduler to generate an optimal schedule.
Therefore, the quality of an online algorithm is often measured by how close it comes to the minimum objective
value in the perfect information case.
There are two main online scheduling paradigms, which are described below. In both models it is common
to assume that the scheduling is done on parallel machines. (i) Online-list, where all jobs are available at time
zero and are ordered in a list/sequence. The jobs are presented to the scheduler one by one following the order
of the list. As soon as a job is presented to the scheduler, he/she learns some or all of its parameter values. The
job then has to be assigned to some machine before the next job is presented and the assignment is irreversible.
(ii) Online-time, where jobs have arbitrary release dates. However, the scheduler is not aware of the existence
of a job until its release date. Note that in both paradigms, the scheduler does not know in advance the number
of jobs to be scheduled.
In some cases, the decision maker may have some knowledge about future jobs. In such cases, the model
is referred to as a semi-online scheduling model. Some of the common semi-online models considered in the
literature assume knowledge of: (i) the total processing time of all jobs (Sum); (ii) the value of the maximum
processing time (Max ); (iii) the value of the optimal solution (Opt); (iv) the order of the jobs presented in the
list (Order ); and (v) the job parameters within a buffer of future jobs (Buffer ).
In order to evaluate the performance of an online (or semi-online) algorithm, it is common to use the
competitive analysis evaluation technique [Sleator & Tarjan, 1985]. Competitive analysis is a type of worst-case
analysis, where the performance of an online algorithm is compared to that of the optimal offline algorithm.
More formally, an online algorithm is δ-competitive if, for each input instance, the objective value of the schedule

is called optimal if its competitive ratio matches the lower bound of the problem.

4.2.

Online and semi-online over list

Consider the online-list paradigm. Many greedy approximation algorithms that have been designed to solve
the offline version of the P ||Cmax problem are based on online decisions (see Section 2.2). Maybe the most
famous example is the LS greedy algorithm by [Graham, 1966]. This algorithm assigns the next job on the list
to the machine that will be the least loaded one as a result of scheduling this job on it (if the machines are
identical this is simply the least loaded machine). This algorithm is (2 − 1/m)-competitive, and is optimal when
m ∈ {2, 3} [Faigle et al., 1989]. [Pruhs et al., 2004] noted that the main disadvantage in Graham’s algorithm
(for m ≥ 4) is in cases where the processing time of the last job is very big, and prior to its assignment all
machines are equally loaded. In such a case, one of the machines will have a completion time much larger
than the others. Therefore, the main idea behind algorithms that improve Graham’s result is to keep a certain
imbalance among the machines such that some machines will have a lighter load than the other machines.
In such a case, if a job with a large processing time arrives, it will be assigned to one of the lightly loaded
machines. By applying this method, better upper bounds have been obtained for m ≥ 4. For example, an
algorithm with a competitive ratio of 1.733 for m = 4 has been presented in [Chen et al., 1994], while the
LS algorithm has a competitive ratio of 1.75 for this case. Still there is a gap, as the best lower bound for

4.4.

Robust scheduling

In this set of problems, either the interval or the multi-scenario approach is used for modeling the uncertainty
in the jobs’ parameters. Recall (see Section 1.3) that in the multi-scenario approach, a set of discrete scenarios
is given, and the values of some of the job parameters are scenario-dependent. In the interval approach, the
value of each uncertain parameter can assume any value within a given interval of values. In principle, any
instance of a problem with intervals (assuming the values of the parameters are restricted to integer values)
can be reduced to an equivalent instance with discrete scenarios, by considering any possible combination of
job parameters taken from the respective intervals as a different scenario. However, such a reduction has the
disadvantage of not necessarily being polynomial in the instance size.
Given an objective function, in robust scheduling the aim is to find a schedule σ that is robust enough for
any scenario realisation, s ∈ S (S is the set of all possible scenarios in the instance). Let F (s, σ) be the value of
schedule σ under scenario s. There are several different interpretations of a robust schedule, each of which leads
to a different variant of the problem. The two most popular are: (i) the Absolute Robustness (AR) problem,
where the aim is to find a schedule σ that minimises maxs∈S {F (s, σ)}; and (ii) the Maximum Regret (MR)
problem, where the aim is to find a schedule σ that minimises maxs∈S {F (s, σ) − F (s, σ ∗ (s))}, where σ ∗ (s) is
the optimal schedule for scenario s. In what follows a brief overview is presented of robust scheduling problems
for the two different approaches (multi-scenario and interval) and for both variants of the problem (AR and
MR).

most classical easy single-scenario scheduling problems become N P-hard when moving to the multi-scenario

Consider now the interval approach. For most classical objective functions (e.g., total weighted completion
time, total weighted tardiness), the objective value is a non-decreasing function of the processing times and
weights and a non-increasing function of the due dates. For any scheduling problem with such an objective
function, the worst scenario for the AR problem occurs when the weights and processing times are at their
maximum value while the due dates are at their minimal value. Therefore, each such problem is equivalent to
its single-scenario counterpart with processing times and weights set to their maximal values, and due dates set
to their minimal values.

5.

Stochastic scheduling models
In stochastic scheduling, either some or all of the input data of an instance of a problem (e.g., processing

times, release dates, due dates, etc.) are random variables whose distributions are known in advance, but the
actual realisations of the random variables only become known during the evolution of the process. For example,
the processing time of a job may be a random variable with only its distribution being known at the beginning of
the process; however the actual processing time of the job only becomes known the moment the job is completed.
The objective function under consideration usually has to be minimised in expectation, denoted by E(·). A
class of decision making policies has to be specified. The specification of such a class determines the types of
actions a decision maker is and is not allowed to take during the evolution of the process. Under a dynamic
policy, a decision maker is allowed to make at any point in time a decision that takes into consideration all the
information that has become available up to that point in time. Several books on stochastic scheduling have
appeared, see [Gittins et al., 2011, Sarin et al., 2014, Cai et al., 2014]. A number of books have some chapters
on this topic as well, see [Dempster et al., 1981, Righter, 1994, Pinedo, 2022].
The notation used in stochastic scheduling has certain similarities to the notation used in deterministic
scheduling, but there are differences. Random variables are in the stochastic literature typically uppercase;
so the random release date, due date, and completion time of job j are usually denoted by Rj , Dj , and Cj ,
respectively. The weight wj of job j is most often considered fixed (not random). However, the processing
time of operation Oij would typically be denoted by the random variable Xij . The distribution function of
the random processing time Xij is denoted by Fij with density function fij . One type of distribution function
that has received an enormous amount of attention in the stochastic scheduling literature is the exponential
distribution. If the random processing time of operation Oij , Xij , has an exponential distribution, then the
rate of this exponential distribution is typically denoted by either λij or µij . The expected processing time of
Oij is then either 1/λij or 1/µij .

The freedom the decision maker has in the actions he/she can take during the evolution of the process is
determined by the class of dynamic policies that is being considered. One may make a distinction between
preemptive and non-preemptive dynamic policies. The methodologies used in stochastic scheduling for determining optimal policies have some overlap with the methodologies used for determining optimal schedules for
offline deterministic models. When the optimal policy is a monotone priority rule, i.e., the jobs are ranked in
increasing or decreasing order of a given function of the jobs’ parameters, then a simple pairwise interchange
argument may suffice to prove optimality. However, at times the optimality of more complicated policies needs
to be shown, requiring more elaborate interchange arguments. When all processing times and other random
variables are exponentially distributed, the scheduling problem can be formulated as a continuous time Markov
Decision Process (MDP). It is well-known that such an MDP can be formulated as a (deterministic) Linear
Program (LP). However, the number of variables and the number of constraints of such an LP may be very
large, see [Pinedo, 2022].

Single Machine

Clearly, among all machine environments the single machine is the one that has received in stochastic

Parallel Machines

Stochastic counterparts of P ||Cmax have received a significant amount of attention. The first processing
time distribution being considered was again the exponential distribution. A number of researchers have shown
independently that the non-preemptive Longest Expected Processing Time first (LEPT) policy is optimal for
the preemptive and non-preemptive stochastic versions of P ||Cmax , i.e., whenever a job has completed its processing on a machine, select among the remaining jobs waiting for processing the one with the longest expected
processing time as the one to be put on the available machine (see [Weiss & Pinedo, 1980], [Bruno et al., 1981],

and [Weiss, 1982]. In a preemptive setting with exponentially distributed processing times, this result could
even be further generalised in other directions: the machines are allowed to have different speeds and the jobs
may be released at different points in time. Preemptions may then take place either when a new job has been
released or when a job has been completed on a machine. At any point in time the job with the longest expected

5.3.

Flow shop, job shop, and open shop scheduling

Another machine environment that has received considerable attention in the stochastic scheduling literature
is the flow shop. The first basic result regarding a stochastic flow shop was about a two-machine flow shop
with job j having an exponentially distributed processing time on machine 1 (2) with rate λj (µj ). Various
authors have shown that the rule that sequences the jobs in decreasing order of λj − µj minimises the expected
makespan, see [Weiss, 1982] and [Ku & Niu, 1986]. This rule exhibits a strong similarity to Johnson’s algorithm
for F 2||Cmax in the deterministic setting (Section 2.3).
Clearly, m-machine stochastic flow shops are extremely hard to analyse. For that reason, researchers have
started to focus on the stochastic counterparts of so-called proportionate flow shops, i.e., F m|pij = pj |γ, assum-

According to a SEPT-LEPT rule, the jobs in the first part of the sequence are ordered according to SEPT,
while the remaining jobs in the second and last part of the sequence are ordered according to LEPT. Such a

the objective function is to minimise the expected total completion time, then under the same fairly strong
form of stochastic dominance the SEPT rule is optimal. Not surprisingly, it is not easy to find any specific
set of conditions for which it can be shown that nice and elegant optimal policies are optimal in stochastic
flow shops. Most researchers have therefore focused on heuristics and approximation algorithms. For example,
[Gourgand et al., 2003] considered the m-machine flow shop with exponentially distributed processing times

algorithm. [Framinan & Perez-Gonzalez, 2015] presented heuristics for the m-machine flow shop problem to
minimise the expected makespan when the jobs have arbitrarily distributed processing times.
Stochastic job shops are clearly harder to analyse than stochastic flow shops. So, fewer results are to be expected. However, the stochastic counterpart of J2||Cmax with exponentially distributed processing times is still

determined optimal policies for special cases of the two-machine open shop problem when the jobs have exponentially distributed processing times.

5.4.

Breakdowns and Repair

A fair amount of research has been done in deterministic settings with machines subject to availability
constraints; for an overview of this research, see [Lee, 2004]. Clearly, the stochastic counterparts of these
models have received a significant amount of attention as well, since in practice breakdowns are often random. [Pinedo & Rammouz, 1988], [Birge et al., 1990], and [Frostig, 1991a] established a variety of different
breakdown conditions under which the preemptive WSERPT rule remains optimal for a single machine with
the total expected weighted completion time as objective. These papers considered various different sets of
assumptions on the breakdown and repair processes as well as different assumptions on the processing time distributions. Moreover, they considered two different modes of operation, namely the preemptive-resume mode
(the job which was preempted by the breakdown is resumed and processed for the remaining processing time)
and the preemptive-repeat mode (the job which was preempted by the breakdown is restarted from scratch).
[Cai & Zhou, 1999] considered a parallel machine model subject to breakdown and repair with jobs having due

6.

Applications of scheduling models in practice
Scheduling is a process that is of critical importance in a wide variety of real world applications. This section

describes some of the more important scheduling applications in manufacturing, in services, and in information
processing. Because of space limitations, the descriptions of these application areas are of a more general nature
and do not go into very specific details with regard to the types of scheduling models that are relevant.

6.1.

Applications in Manufacturing

In many manufacturing and production environments, scheduling processes have been studied in detail. Such
manufacturing settings can often be modeled as flexible job shops with multiple objectives. One of the most
P
important objectives in manufacturing settings is the total weighted tardiness, i.e., wj Tj , since orders typically
have predetermined shipping dates which are basically equivalent to due dates. One manufacturing environment
that has received an enormous amount of attention in the scheduling literature is the semiconductor industry
[Márquez & Ribeiro, 2022]. A semiconductor manufacturing environment, e.g., a wafer fab, has typically a
number of special features. The machine environment is usually a flexible job shop in which jobs have to
recirculate, i.e., a job may have to visit any given machine or work center more than once. Also, jobs are often
subject to sequence-dependent setup times (denoted by sjk in the β field). A relevant deterministic scheduling
P
model for such an environment would be F J|rcrc, sjk | wj Tj (the rcrc in the β field indicates that a job
may recirculate). Moreover, in semiconductor manufacturing settings batch scheduling plays an important
role. The heuristics developed in practice for wafer fab scheduling are highly complex and typically based on
decomposition approaches (see [Pfund et al., 2006, Moench et al., 2011] for surveys on this topic).
Another important manufacturing environment where scheduling plays an important role is the steel industry.
Steelmaking and continuous casting are important stages in a steel mill’s production process and they do require
very careful scheduling. An integrated process of steelmaking, continuous casting and hot rolling comprises a
synchronised production process that links the steelmaking furnace, the continuous caster, and the rolling mill.
This production process can typically be modeled as a flexible flow shop with jobs (i.e., orders) that have fixed
P
due dates, i.e., a model based on F F |sjk | wj Tj . There are several objectives to be minimised, including the
total weighted tardiness and the waiting times of the jobs in between the successive stages of production. The
latter objective arises because during a waiting period a job may cool off and that would then require reheating
before entering the next stage. [Tang et al., 2001] presented a detailed survey of all the scheduling processes in
steelmaking and continuous casting operations.

Production scheduling processes that also take power consumption and energy management into account
have become in recent years an important area of research. Because of the highly variable renewable power
that is nowadays part of the energy supply mix, energy pricing has become a function of time. In order to
make a production process as efficient and as economical as possible, the scheduling problems that are being
analysed take energy costs into account as well. Basically, this requires the energy demand to shift from periods
in which there is peak energy demand to periods in which there is low energy demand. One may have to
distinguish then between jobs that are delay-sensitive and jobs that are delay-tolerant. When energy costs are
being incurred and vary over time, the problem is to schedule tasks in the most appropriate time slots (this is
referred to as time-of-use tariffs, see [Chen & Zhang, 2019, Catanzaro et al., 2023]). Similar problems arise in
smart homes, where appliance scheduling is one of the crucial components of home energy management systems
[Zhou et al., 2016]. For surveys on this topic, see [Merkert et al., 2015] and [Duarte et al., 2020].
Scheduling is important in many other manufacturing settings as well. For example, scheduling plays a
major role in the programming of robotic cells in manufacturing. A robot typically has to perform a sequence of activities over time with movements that require transportation times in between. For a robot to
function as efficiently as possible, often a solution to an N P-hard scheduling problem has to be found. The
book by [Dawande et al., 2007] and the surveys by [Brauner, 2008], [Levner et al., 2010] and [Feng et al., 2024]
provided a detailed overview of this application area.

6.2.

Applications in Service Industries

Scheduling plays an important role in the services industries as well. One of the most important service
industries is the health care industry, and a class of important scheduling problems in health care is appointment
scheduling. Appointment scheduling is usually equivalent to either a single machine or a parallel-machine
scheduling problem (a service provider is basically equivalent to a machine). Requests for appointments may
come in at random points in time. An appointment (i.e., a job) may have to be made within a given time window
(i.e., determined by a release date and a due date) and has to be inserted into an open slot within that time
window in a (partial) schedule. Appointment scheduling may therefore also be regarded as a form of interval
scheduling. However, one very important feature of appointment scheduling in health care is the probability
that a person, who had made an appointment, does not show up. These no-show probabilities do embed some
stochasticity in the problem. A no-show may cause the service provider to be idle for a certain amount of time
and the service provider may therefore decide to overbook. The overall objective may be a weighted combination
of the total expected weighted tardiness and the total expected idle time. Over the years several survey papers
have appeared reviewing appointment scheduling research, see [Cayirli & Veral, 2003, Gupta & Denton, 2008,
Ahmadi-Javad et al., 2017, Dantas et al., 2018].

Another important form of scheduling in hospital settings is surgery scheduling, which is also an application
of stochastic scheduling. A hospital typically has a number of operating rooms, which are basically equivalent to
a bank of parallel machines. In a particular operating room, on any given day, a number of surgeries have to be
performed one after another. These surgeries may have to be performed either by the same team or by different
teams. A surgery scheduling problem is, for several reasons, a stochastic scheduling problem. First, surgery times
are usually random, often following a known distribution (e.g., the Normal distribution); second, there is in this
setting also a probability of no-show (for one reason or another a surgery may have to be cancelled or postponed);
third, there may at times be an unexpected arrival of an emergency patient that has to be inserted in the schedule,
thereby postponing the surgeries that would follow. The objective function in operating room scheduling usually
consists of two components. One component concerns the idle time of the operating room. Such an idle time
may be caused either by an unexpected cancellation of a surgery or by a particular surgery taking less time than
originally anticipated. The second component concerns the time a surgical team has to wait for the operating
room to become available for its surgery. Such a waiting time may be caused either by a particular surgery
taking significantly longer than originally anticipated (thus delaying the next surgery), or by the insertion of an
unexpected emergency surgery in the schedule. Surgical scheduling problems are often addressed in combination
with other relevant, higher-level problems, such as selecting the surgeries to be performed on a given day (often
called the surgical case assignment problem), or assigning operating room sessions to surgical specialties (often
called the master surgical scheduling problem). Several survey papers have appeared reviewing surgery and
operating room scheduling, see, e.g., [Cardoen et al., 2010, May et al., 2011, Rahimi & Gandomi, 2021].
Another services industry where scheduling plays an important role is the transportation industry. For
example, in vehicle routing the vehicles play the role of the machines that have to visit a number of locations
to deliver their goods. A distance matrix is given and optimal routes have to be found for the vehicles. This
type of problem is equivalent to a parallel machine scheduling problem with the distances being equivalent to
sequence dependent setup times. The processing times are equivalent to the loading and unloading times at
the various locations. In vehicle routing time windows may play a role as well. If a delivery has to be made
within a given time window, that would correspond then to the particular processing time being subject to
a given release date and due date. The objective in such a problem may also consist of various components.
First, if there are time windows, then a due date-related objective function may play a role. Second, there
is also a desire to balance the workload over the machines (i.e., vehicles and drivers), which implies that the
makespan may be part of the objective function. A deterministic scheduling problem that could be similar to
P
such a model would be P m|sjk , rj | wj Tj + αCmax . Several survey papers have been written on this topic (see,
e.g., [Solomon & Desrosiers, 1988]).

Another major application area in transportation is railway scheduling. These problems can often be modeled

found in [Ribeiro et al., 2023].

6.3.

Applications in Information Processing

Scheduling plays an important role in the information processing industry as well. One form of information
processing is grid computing which involves the use of widely distributed computer resources for solving very
large jobs and provide non-trivial services to users. Grid scheduling models have several features that make
them hard to analyse. First, the environment is dynamic with a high level of stochasticity due to random arrivals
of jobs. Their random processing times are very hard to predict. The basic processing environment assumes
a (large) number of resources in parallel which operate at different speeds, i.e., they function as a bank of
uniform machines. Resources can be computers, processors (CPU, GPU) with changing performances, storage
space, access to data, etc. Resources may be owned by several organisations and under certain conditions be
shared, which makes multi-agent scheduling models highly relevant. A job (also called metatask) may typically
be partitioned into smaller (atomic) subtasks that may be subject to precedence constraints and that may
have to be assigned to different resources. A task is generally characterised by CPU, memory requirements,
a deadline and a priority. In the final stage of the scheduling process the results obtained for the subtasks
have to be reassembled to generate a solution for the original job. The specific problem underlying the grid
computing concept is on the one hand the coordinated assignment to resources and on the other hand the
resolution of problems in a dynamic and multi-agent organisation. In fact, in the context of grid scheduling, a

sort of extended notion of multi-agent scheduling (see Section 3.2) has been proposed, called multiorganisation
scheduling [Pascual et al., 2009]. In this case the agents (here called organisations) own both a set of tasks
and a set of parallel machines. The tasks of an agent can be run on another agent’s machine, as long as this
does not increase the hosting agent’s makespan, and there are a system-wide objective and agents’ individual
objectives. Such a sharing of machines and tasks can benefit all the organisations simultaneously, as shown by
several approximation and fairness results [Durand & Pascual, 2021].
Among many special features of scheduling problems in information processing, one which has received a lot
of attention concerns malleable tasks, i.e., tasks which may be executed by several processors simultaneously at a
speed which depends on the number of processors allocated to the task. Many results for the malleable version of
P ||Cmax exist, e.g., the problem is easy when n ≤ m (for many different speed functions) [Blazewicz et al., 2004],
while in general it is N P-hard [Leung, 2004a].
In the last few decades, scheduling models for computer systems has increasingly incorporated energy issues.
In fact, higher processing speeds (resulting in improved time performance) imply higher energy consumption and
higher temperatures, possibly causing technical problems related to device cooling. Hence, new problems (both
offline and online, and typically preemptive) address energy-related objectives along with classical scheduling
objectives and constraints, such as time windows which need to be met. In most of these models, selecting the
processor speed for each job is part of the decision. While, in the offline setting, [Yao et al., 1995] provided an
algorithm that minimises the energy required by a set of tasks, [Bansal et al., 2007] provided several competitive
ratio results with respect to energy, maximum speed, and maximum power.
Since high performance computing and information processing environments consume tremendous amounts
of energy, energy-aware scheduling has become an important area of research in these settings as well. The
models studied in this area exhibit some similarities with the energy-aware scheduling models arising in manufacturing settings. Various different types of objectives play a role: First, the Quality of Service (QoS)
which may be closely related to the total expected weighted completion time of all jobs or, equivalently, the
total expected weighted flow time. Second, the utilisation of the resources has to be maximised, which is
equivalent to a minimisation of the idle times of the machines. Third, the workload over the various resources has to be balanced. For reviews of scheduling processes and algorithms in this application area, see
e.g., [Nabrzyski et al., 2004, Dong & Akl, 2006, Drozdowski, 2009, Kocot et al., 2023].

Conclusions and future trends
This survey presents an overview of classical deterministic scheduling problems, including single machine,

parallel machines and shop scheduling problems. Its focus encompasses also more general problems including
multicriteria problems, multi-agent problems, and other problems, such as scheduling with rejection and with

7

controllable processing times as well as due date assignment problems, just-in-time problems, time-dependent
scheduling, and problems with batching and setups. It also addresses robust, online and semi-online scheduling
problems and it reviews the main results in stochastic scheduling in single machine, parallel machines and shop
environments. It furthermore describes a number of applications that appear to be important in practice. Of
course, other more specialised areas could have been addressed as well, such as scheduling with unavailability
periods, scheduling with a common server, assembly line scheduling, and others. More application areas could
have been mentioned as well, such as multi-skill scheduling problems, workforce scheduling, nurse rostering and
quay crane scheduling.
From the previous sections it is already clear that there are still many open problems remaining in the existing
scheduling frameworks and paradigms, i.e., in offline scheduling, online scheduling, and stochastic scheduling.
At times new theoretical frameworks, new paradigms, and new types of heuristic procedures are introduced in
the literature. Such new developments may either be hybrid of existing paradigms and techniques or they may
actually embody a very new and original concept. In any new framework there are, of course, a plethora of new
problems to be investigated.
Regarding future research in the theoretical aspects of scheduling, there are new areas to be explored
with regard to more refined complexity classes, in addition to the existing problems for which the complexity status has not been determined yet. For example, the area of Fixed Parameter Tractability (FPT) has
started to gain a lot of attention in recent years (see, e.g., [Mnich & Wiese, 2015], [Hermelin et al., 2021],
[Hanen & Munier-Kordon, 2024] and [Heeger & Hermelin, 2024]). Fixed-parameter algorithms assume that
some parameters of the problem are bounded and have a time complexity function of type f (k) × p(n), where
k is a given problem parameter, f a superpolynomial function of k and p(n) a polynomial function of n. Like
in classical complexity theory, given a problem and a parameter, one may also be able to rule out the existence
of an FPT algorithm (under certain assumptions) through reductions, e.g., by proving that the problem (with
a given parameter) belongs to the so-called W[1]-hard set of problems (see, e.g., [Downey & Fellows, 2013] for
more details). [Mnich & Van Bevern, 2018] have presented seminal results with regard to the parameterised
complexity of scheduling problems and provided a list of 15 open problems.
Other methods to provide lower bounds on the time complexity required to solve combinatorial problems
arise from time to time. In addition to classical hypotheses (e.g., P =
̸ N P), one can now use other new
and widely acceptable hypotheses, such as the strong exponential time hypothesis (SETH) or alternatively
the (min,+)-conjecture. These hypotheses have just started to gain the attention of theoretical schedulers.
For example, assuming SETH holds, [Jansen & Kahler, 2023] proved that the famous Lawler and Moore DP
P
algorithm [Lawler & Moore, 1969] is the best possible for the problems 1|| wj Uj and P m||Cmax . Lastly, the
APX complexity classification of many N P-hard problems is still open and worth further investigation.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE TRENDS

50

There are two paradigms of online scheduling that have recently been considered in the literature. The first
is stochastic online scheduling, which combines the main characteristics of online and stochastic scheduling (see,
e.g., [Megow et al., 2006, Schulz, 2008]). In this paradigm, job processing times are assumed to be stochastic,
but in contrast to traditional stochastic scheduling models, it is now assumed that the jobs arrive online. Once
a job arrives, the scheduler knows some of its characteristics, including its processing time distribution. Similar
to other online scheduling paradigms, there is no knowledge about jobs that will arrive in the future. The second paradigm is scheduling with prediction (see, e.g., [Lattanzi et al., 2020, Boyar et al., 2023, Im et al., 2023,
Balkanski et al., 2024]), where jobs arrive in an online fashion, and there are some predictions about the parameters of future jobs. Moreover, there is some knowledge about the quality of the prediction. Here the objective
is to design an online algorithm that exploits the predictions and has a minimum possible competitive ratio
with respect to the accuracy of the prediction. The study of stochastic online problems and online scheduling
with prediction can find many applications in practice, and are both only in the early stages of research. We
believe that these two extensions of online scheduling may capture a lot of interest in the near future.
Multi-agent scheduling is also a relevant topic that can be generalised in various directions, e.g., by considering agents who can cooperate. In such cases Nash equilibria may be of interest. Notions concerning
fairness in scheduling are relatively new [Agnetis et al., 2019] and deserve to be investigated in this domain as
well. [Hermelin et al., 2025] presented a new generic framework that allows the modeling of numerous repetitive scheduling problems where fairness is the main criterion under consideration. In this set of problems the
same set of clients submits jobs to be processed on the same set of machines on periodic (e.g., daily) basis. In
such a model a client that receives a poor schedule on one day can be compensated on the other days. The
objective is to obtain a fair set of schedules that ensures the highest possible QoS for the least satisfied client.
The analysis in [Hermelin et al., 2025] focuses on the single machine case with performance measures being
the completion time, waiting time and lateness, and can be extended in many ways including: other machine
environments and/or performance measures, other notions of fairness and a more comprehensive analysis of
the PoF. [Varadi & Haskell, 2025] considered a model in which each agent’s utility is a concave function of the
time the agent is given exclusive access to an indivisible resource. They identify fairness with envy-freeness and
investigate how the price of fairness depends on various system parameters. Their analysis can be extended in
several directions by changing assumptions, and it opens the way to new approximation and complexity results.
Also, scenarios can be investigated in which agents have different roles, similarly to a Stackelberg game setting
(see e.g., adversarial bilevel scheduling in [T’kindt et al., 2024]).
With regard to solution methodologies, the development of new techniques in recent years has made it possible to solve large scale problems in practice (sometimes with tens of thousands of activities and hundreds of
resources) within reasonable computation times. It has become obvious that most scheduling problems in many

7

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE TRENDS

51

industries have multiple objectives, and efficient solution techniques have to be found for such scheduling problems. New heuristics have to be developed for multi-objective scheduling problems that will enable a decision
maker to perform a trade-off analysis and find Pareto-optimal schedules in an efficient manner. Search techniques that now integrate metaheuristic algorithms with mathematical programming or constraint programming
procedures result in powerful optimisation tools that are very promising [Lin & Gen, 2018]. It has to be noted
that machine learning – and in particular, reinforcement learning (RL) – is being increasingly adopted as a valuable approach to address scheduling problems. However, it is only in the last few years that academic research
has started to focus on the application of machine learning in production systems and more particularly in job
shop scheduling and in assembly problems, using reinforcement learning and deep reinforcement learning, see
[Panzer & Bender, 2022] or [Huang et al., 2024]. These algorithms have shown their effectiveness in finding solutions of comparable quality under current benchmarks within less computation time [Kayhan & Yildiz, 2023],
and in finding robust solutions to problems with a high level of uncertainty. However, further experiments with
these algorithms have to be conducted to validate their effectiveness.
Another solution technique that is commonly applied to solving N P-hard scheduling problems is based on
modifying a pseudo polynomial time algorithm into an FPTAS. The construction of FPTAS’s for scheduling
problems dates back to 1976 [Sahni, 1976], which presented three different methods to do so. Ever since, these
three techniques have been applied to many problems. [Halman et al., 2014, Alon & Halman, 2021] presented
a new method to construct FPTAS’s. This method is based on K-approximation sets and functions, and was
found useful for improving the time complexity of various FPTAS’s presented in the literature. This method
can be used to improve many other FPTAS’s. It seems that the strong connections between half product
minimisation and scheduling problems also lead to the design of many FPTAS’s for scheduling problems in
various areas, including scheduling on parallel machines, JIT scheduling, scheduling with controllable processing
times, scheduling with rejection and scheduling with unavailability periods [Kellerer et al., 2016]. We will not
be surprised if those connections will yield many more results in the future.
With regard to scheduling applications in practice, many domains deserve more attention. A scheduling
problem is rarely static with all machines being available from time zero on and constraints fully defined.
More generally, it is a continuous and iterative process during which unexpected and unpredictable events may
arise that disrupt the production environment. Considering a scheduling problem in its dynamic and reactive
context can open many new application areas, where proposing a flexible (adaptable) and robust solution is
more important than a near-optimal solution that has a very limited chance of being applied. Distributed
and agent-based planning models arise in areas where problem characteristics (e.g., geographic separation)
prevent the development of centralised solutions. The question of how to coordinate the use of resources across
multiple distributed processes as efficiently as possible is still little studied. A scheduling problem is rarely

REFERENCES

52

one that does not depend on other decision making processes. Often, upstream of the scheduling problem
decisions have to be made with regard to capacity planning. Downstream of the production process finished
products have to be shipped to customers [Chen & Hall, 2022]. In the distribution of finished products drone
delivery has begun to play an important role. This has led to a new area in scheduling research, namely drone
scheduling, see [Pasha et al., 2022]. The integration of scheduling problems with planning or routing problems
typically leads to highly complex problems for which mathematical solvers do not appear to be very efficient,
see [Lenstra & Shmoys, 2021].
In summary, it appears that scheduling theory is an increasingly active field of research, where problems of
a very wide variety are of great scientific interest, both from a theoretical point of view, but also in terms of
applications.
