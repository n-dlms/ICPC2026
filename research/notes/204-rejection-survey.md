# 204 — Survey on offline scheduling with rejection (Shabtay, Gaspar, Kaspi 2013) — [SECOND-HAND]

> Unavailable note: Springer (Journal of Scheduling), paywalled; only an abstract-level free copy exists (academia.edu, login-walled). Summary is second-hand from the abstract, the BGU research-portal record, and the reference lists of freely available citing papers (Mor & Shapira arXiv:1711.03720; Zhang/Lu/Yuan EJOR 2009; Chinese survey 2020). Claim-level details not independently verified.

**Claim.** A unified survey of the offline "scheduling with rejection" landscape (mostly single machine): a job is either processed or rejected for a penalty; objective = regular criterion over accepted jobs + rejection penalties. It maps which problems are polynomial, weakly NP-hard (pseudo-polynomial DP), or strongly NP-hard, gives FPTAS results, and highlights the equivalence between rejection and *controllable processing times* and *due-date assignment* models.

**Relevance.** Sets expectations for our port-planning: with rejection available, *most* deadline-flavored variants are NP-hard, so any exact offline re-optimization (doc-05 P2 "full-information re-optimization") must be DP/heuristic, not a polynomial oracle. Specifically: single-machine 1|r_j|ΣU_j (maximize on-time with release times) is strongly NP-hard §S (Elements of Scheduling ch.
5 §S confirms: 1|r_j|ΣU_j strongly NP-hard even though 1||ΣU_j is O(n log n)); two-machine flow shop with rejection is NP-hard (Shabtay & Gasper, COR 39(5):1087–1096, 2012 §S) — the closest published relative to our two-site setting. On the positive side: equal-processing-time variants, makespan+rejection with release dates admit FPTAS/PTAS; unweighted 1||ΣU_j is exactly Moore–Hodgson (note 202); weighted 1||Σw_jU_j is NP-hard.

**Algorithm sketch (patterns, §S).** For single machine with rejection and a sum criterion: sort by a key, knapsack-style DP over (total accepted work, total rejection cost) — e.g., the DP recurrence in Mor & Shapira arXiv:1711.03720 for 1|r_j|makespan+rejection-cost-with-cap; FPTAS by standard rounding. For 1|r_j|ΣU_j-style problems: NP-hard ⇒ practical route is greedy admission (MH/SPT variants, notes 202/205) plus local search, not exact solves.

**Port plan.** No direct code.
Feeds architecture: (a) justifies *keeping admission as a heuristic pre-filter* — an exact "admit subset that maximizes on-time completions" solver would be NP-hard in the full model (deadlines + windows + two resources only make it harder); (b) the survey's rejection ⇔ controllable-processing-time equivalence suggests a *graceful-degradation* variant: if a request cannot make D, estimate whether shrinking its decode rate (fewer concurrent tokens) saves enough for others — in our protocol this means deliberately *not* granting a doomed-but-alive request's tokens so capacity goes to winners (currently `doomed` only stops *new* work; there is no mid-flight token cutoff — a candidate experiment: mark `starve` on requests whose remaining-token bound already exceeds D, see grant_decodes line 107 check).

**Weaknesses (transfer gaps).**

- Survey is *offline*; our judge is online-interactive. Offline NP-hardness does not imply online hardness, and online competitive results (notes 201/206) are the relevant yardstick for the live loop.
- Almost all surveyed models have no communication delays, no two-resource windows, no token-granularity decode — the complexity results are for much simpler machines; the *hardness* direction transfers (our problem embeds those), the *algorithms* mostly do not.
- Rejection penalties are explicit and tunable; ours are implicit and uniform (every request is worth exactly 1 on-time point). Papers' trade-off "accept vs pay penalty" becomes "admit vs hope for a better request later" — a different, harder decision.
