## Candidate techniques

Prefill and decode scheduling shared useful features with LLM inference serving:
staged work, batching, resource contention, and latency targets
[[63](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21901v1), [332](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3589639)].
This note evaluated five candidate changes I considered for the solver: prefill chunking,
dynamic decode batching, upload admission control, local-task priority, and
cloud placement. Each required validation against the problem's transfer FIFO,
setup costs, and score formula before adoption, and I adopted only what passed my gates.

First, implementing **Sarathi-style chunked prefills** addresses the critical issue of Cloud computer underutilization. By splitting the long-running `P_PROC` task into smaller pieces, the scheduler can interleave other tasks like `D_PRE` and `D_POST`, preventing a single request's input stage from monopolizing the remote server [[87](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/agrawal), [220](https://wentao.site/chunked_prefill_sarathi_summary_2/)].

This directly tackles the suspected gap in pipelining and resource overlap, offering a high expected impact on throughput without requiring preemptive execution.

The condition for splitting is favorable given the non-zero schedule cost `S ≥ 1`.

Second, developing **dynamic decode batching logic** moves beyond the current solver's flawed EOQ-based policy, which can only cap a batch size downwards. Inspired by systems like FairBatching and AugServe, this approach would adjust the target batch size in real-time based on system pressure metrics derived from computer busy times and queue lengths [[122](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14392v1), [123](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.04013)]. This provides a more sophisticated trade-off between maximizing token throughput (`tp`) and minimizing waiting times (`TDR`, `TPOT`), directly targeting both components of the scoring function.

Third, introducing a **queue-length-based admission controller** refines the simplistic `PPRE_MAX_INFLIGHT` cap. Drawing from networking principles like Nagle's algorithm and queueing theory, this policy would regulate the rate of new requests entering the system by monitoring the length of the UP transfer FIFO queue [[80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagle%27s_algorithm), [154](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10535934)]. Throttling admissions when the queue grows prevents upstream congestion that currently appears to degrade performance, particularly in TDR-constrained scenarios.

Fourth, formalizing the **local-task priority using a cμ-like index** replaces the ad-hoc priority logic with a mathematically grounded principle. The `cμ` rule, a cornerstone of queueing theory, prioritizes tasks based on their urgency (a derivative of the score's penalty for delay) multiplied by their service rate [[11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304414921000326), [12](https://www.politesi.polimi.it/bitstream/10589/139990/3/2018_04_Invernizzi_Ripanti.pdf)]. Applying this to the choice between `P_POST`, `D_PRE`, and other ready tasks creates a closed feedback loop that dynamically adapts to the system's current bottleneck, improving responsiveness and overall efficiency.

Finally, applying the **power-of-two-choices for load balancing** offers a simple yet effective enhancement to the remote assignment strategy. Since the output length `Lout` is unknown at the time of assignment, assigning a request to the least-loaded of two randomly selected remotes is a robust heuristic that often outperforms deterministic minimum-estimated-completion-time approaches, leading to better overall load distribution across the K cloud computers.

These were separate experiments I ran. I passed no change into the shipped solver without it clearing my correctness and
score gates.

## Catalogue of Applicable Techniques from Scheduling Theory and Queueing Models

The ICPC 2026 scheduling problem presents a constrained online flow shop scenario with shared transport resources, making foundational results from scheduling theory and queueing models highly relevant [[17](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rwh/students/spoonhower.pdf), [202](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10891/25974)].

These theories provide principled rules for task ordering, resource allocation, and managing queues, which can inform the design of more robust policies for the five decision knobs.

While some advanced theorems may have limited direct applicability due to non-preemption and unknown job sizes, their underlying principles offer powerful heuristics.

The primary challenge lies in adapting these offline-optimal or clairvoyant-centric ideas to an online, reactive environment where decisions must be made with incomplete information [[254](https://opus4.kobv.de/opus4-matheon/files/540/5465_b13-sos-prec.pdf), [257](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/51480366/RM09052.pdf)].

One of the most fundamental concepts is the Shortest-Remaining-Processing-Time (SRPT) discipline, which is proven to minimize mean response time in many scheduling contexts [[306](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07248v2), [360](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_YGn6WkwWfI)].

However, SRPT is inherently preemptive, and the problem's non-preemptive constraint makes a direct port impossible [[32](https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/operating-systems/preemptive-and-non-preemptive-scheduling/), [357](https://web.stanford.edu/~ouster/cgi-bin/cs140-spring19/lecture.php?topic=scheduling)].

Nonetheless, the principle behind SRPT—that shorter jobs should generally be favored—can be approximated. Since the duration of the `P_PROC` stage is known (based on `Lin`), a greedy policy could prioritize requests with shorter prefill times for immediate processing. For the decode stage, where `Lout` is unknown, this becomes an approximation.

A related concept is the Gittins index, which provides an optimal scheduling policy for jobs with unknown, stochastically distributed processing times [[33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7yf-fBEGSqA), [34](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10872v2)]. The Gittins index assigns a value to each job based on its potential future reward, allowing the scheduler to choose the job that maximizes expected utility [[39](https://www.columbia.edu/~cs2035/courses/ieor4405.S16/gittins.pdf)].

While calculating the exact Gittins index can be complex, its conceptual framework suggests prioritizing requests that are either very urgent (high penalty for delay) or are likely to complete quickly, even if their total size is unknown. This is a powerful alternative to simple FIFO or round-robin approaches for the decode queue.

Queueing theory offers another rich source of inspiration, particularly through the lens of the decode loop as a bulk-service queue [[49](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273399090_Analysis_of_a_finite-buffer_bulk-service_queue_under_Markovian_arrival_process_with_batch-size-dependent_service), [70](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261514000307)].

The Pollaczek–Khinchine formula for M/G/1 queues highlights that waiting time increases with the variance of service times [[203](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c8xijdNhNcc)]. This implies that having a few very large decode batches might lead to longer average waiting times than many medium-sized batches, even if the total number of tokens is the same.

This connects directly to the open question about monotonicity of throughput with respect to batch size. It is unlikely to be monotonic, especially with clamped task-time tables; a very large batch might cause a decode step to take significantly longer than the sum of its parts, increasing TPOT and hurting the score.

The Kingman's approximation extends this insight, showing that heavy traffic (near saturation) exacerbates the impact of variance [[203](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c8xijdNhNcc)]. Therefore, a scheduler should aim to keep the system away from heavy traffic by controlling batch sizes.

The cμ-rule is arguably one of the most directly applicable principles from queueing theory [[12](https://www.politesi.polimi.it/bitstream/10589/139990/3/2018_04_Invernizzi_Ripanti.pdf)]. It states that for a multi-class queueing system, the optimal policy to minimize holding costs is to serve the customer class with the highest product of its holding cost (`c`) and its service rate (`μ`).

In this context, we can map the problem's components to fit the cμ-rule. The "holding cost" (`c`) corresponds to the marginal penalty of delaying a request. This can be estimated from the score's gradient with respect to TDR and TPOT. For instance, if `w_c` is high, the cost of increasing TDR or TPOT is also high.

The "service rate" (`μ`) is the inverse of the remaining service time for a task. For a decode batch of size `m`, `μ` is proportional to `1 / decode_time(m)`. For a specific piece of a `P_PROC` task, `μ` is constant.

The rule suggests choosing the ready task (e.g., the next piece of a specific `P_PROC`, or a decode batch of a certain size) that maximizes `c * μ`. This provides a rigorous, quantitative basis for the local-task priority decision, moving beyond ad-hoc heuristics.

The applicability is complicated by setup costs (the schedule cost `S` and transfer latencies) and unknown job sizes (`Lout`), but simplified versions of the rule remain powerful guides [[11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304414921000326), [13](http://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/VanMieghem/htm/gcmu0.pdf)]. Learning-based variants of the cμ-rule have been proposed to handle unknown service rates, which could be adapted to estimate the service time of a decode batch based on past observations [[14](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06723), [153](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06077e1d71f5661318dafb07a1bd3524741014fc)].

The problem of deciding whether to hold a `D_PRE` task to accumulate a larger batch is analogous to the "ski rental problem," a classic online decision-making problem that balances the cost of waiting against the cost of acting now. The scheduler must decide whether to run a small batch immediately or wait to see if more requests arrive, risking increased idle time. The solution involves comparing the marginal gain in throughput from a larger batch against the marginal loss in score from the increased waiting time. This is precisely what the current solver attempts with its `SloAwareDelay` policy, though it uses a static alpha scalar. A more dynamic approach, inspired by online algorithms, could base the decision on real-time metrics like the length of the ready queue and the observed arrival rate of new requests.

Finally, the entire system can be viewed as a tandem queue, where requests move sequentially through different stages [[202](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/10891/25974)]. Heavy-traffic approximations and diffusion models can predict the system's behavior as it approaches capacity, helping to identify the bottleneck resource (Edge CPU, Cloud CPU, UP link, DOWN link). The current solver already accumulates busy-time statistics for each resource, which can be used as a feedback signal in a control-theoretic regulator (like a PI or PID controller) to dynamically adjust parameters such as the maximum decode batch size or the admission rate [[81](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.21364v1)]. This would create a closed-loop system that self-tunes to maintain balance and avoid bottlenecks, addressing the identified gap of having no feedback loop from live utilization to decisions.

## Adaptation of LLM Serving System Strategies for Pipeline Optimization

The ICPC 2026 problem is a scheduling model with similarities to LLM serving systems, where disaggregation of prefill and decode computations is common [[63](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21901v1)]. Prefill corresponds to the initial KV-cache generation, which is compute-intensive, while decode involves iterative token generation, which is memory-bound and benefits immensely from batching [[3](https://mellerikat.com/en/blog_tech/Research/vllm), [260](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388920244_Online_Scheduling_for_LLM_Inference_with_KV_Cache_Constraints)]. Consequently, the extensive body of research on optimizing LLM serving provides a set of potentially relevant techniques. These systems have solved similar problems of throughput maximization, latency minimization, and resource co-scheduling, offering concrete, named solutions that can be mapped to the problem's decision knobs.

One candidate is **chunked prefills**, pioneered by systems like Sarathi-Serve [[87](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/agrawal), [220](https://wentao.site/chunked_prefill_sarathi_summary_2/)]. In standard scheduling, a prefill request is processed as a single, monolithic task. However, since `P_PROC` can be split into pieces, the scheduler can break it up. This allows the Edge computer to interleave `D_PRE` or `P_POST` tasks for other requests between the pieces of a single `P_PROC` [[87](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/agrawal)]. This effectively creates a form of manual pipeline parallelism, where the decode stage does not have to wait for the entire prefill to finish before starting. This directly addresses the user's suspicion about a lack of pipelining and a fixed local-task priority. The benefit comes from amortizing the schedule cost `S` over smaller units of work; if `S` is significant relative to the total prefill time, breaking the job into `k` chunks reduces the effective idle time between pieces [[115](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/27093)]. The optimal number of chunks is a trade-off between reducing the gap between chunks and incurring the scheduling overhead of `k` separate tasks. This technique is feasible within the problem's non-preemptive constraints because the pieces must be processed in order, which is already a requirement.

Another relevant technique is **continuous batching**, introduced by Orca [[60](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17768v2), [233](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14966v1)]. Traditional schedulers use static batching, where a batch is formed, executed to completion, and then discarded. Orca's innovation was to schedule at the granularity of a single decode iteration, allowing the composition of batches to evolve over time [[287](https://www.cse.iitd.ac.in/~rijurekha/col851/scheduling_oct23_oct25/2022_osdi-orca.pdf)]. This means a request does not need to wait for all other requests in a batch to finish; a decode step can be removed from a batch once its corresponding token is produced. More importantly, this enables hybrid batches that mix prefill and decode requests, and it allows new requests to be added into the space vacated by finished decode steps [[134](https://www.kunwar.page/chapter/023-batching-static-dynamic-continuous-orca), [167](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14966v2)]. For the ICPC problem, this translates to a more flexible batching policy. Instead of forming a decode batch and running it as a single atomic unit, the scheduler could admit a new request's first decode step into an ongoing `D_PROC` group. The problem's rules permit combining requests from different remotes in a `D_PRE` task, which is the necessary condition for this flexibility. Implementing a version of this would require the scheduler to track not just completed `P_POST`s but also the progress of active decode batches, allowing new requests to be admitted into the `D_PRE` phase earlier. This improves responsiveness and can significantly boost throughput, especially under bursty arrivals.

Beyond these core innovations, several other LLM-serving techniques are highly relevant. **KV-cache-aware routing**, used in systems like llm-d, directs incoming requests to accelerators that already hold relevant KV-cache data for a prefix of the request, reducing prefill computation and transfer time [[258](https://llm-d.ai/blog/kvcache-wins-you-can-see), [261](https://developers.redhat.com/articles/2025/10/07/master-kv-cache-aware-routing-llm-d-efficient-ai-inference)]. While our problem doesn't have a cache hit ratio, a similar principle can be applied. The load balancer could prioritize remotes that already have a similar `Lin` value queued, assuming this might correlate with faster processing. Another technique is **stall-free scheduling**, which aims to keep all computational resources saturated [[363](https://huggingface.co/papers/2403.02310)]. Sarathi-Serve explicitly designs schedules to avoid stalls, for example, by overlapping transfers with computation wherever possible. The current solver already overlaps transfers and compute, but a stall-free philosophy would push this further, perhaps by being more aggressive in starting decode batches even if they are not yet at their "ideal" size, to prevent the Cloud computers from idling. Finally, **throughput-latency Pareto frontier** exploration is a key theme in the field [[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02310v2), [114](https://github.com/deepjavalibrary/djl-demo/blob/master/aws/sagemaker/large-model-inference/sample-llm/vllm-qwen3-inference-optimization.ipynb)]. Systems like Sarathi-Serve demonstrate that by carefully tuning scheduling policies, one can operate efficiently along this frontier, trading off latency for higher throughput. This aligns perfectly with the problem's objective of balancing `norm_tp` and `norm_c`.

## Advanced Control Policies for Admission, Load Balancing, and Task Prioritization

While LLM-serving strategies provide excellent guidance for pipeline structure, more advanced control-theoretic and game-theoretic approaches offer principled methods for managing the system's flow and internal dynamics. These techniques address three critical areas of the current solver's weaknesses: admission control, load balancing under uncertainty, and task prioritization. They shift the paradigm from simple heuristics to closed-loop, feedback-driven control systems, which are better equipped to handle the problem's dynamic and stochastic nature.

For **admission control**, the current solver's `PPRE_MAX_INFLIGHT` cap is a rudimentary back-pressure mechanism. A queue-aware approach can be derived from networking and queueing theory. The problem of when to accept a new request is analogous to the "ski rental problem," where one must decide whether to commit to a course of action or wait. In this context, the scheduler must decide whether to initiate a new request's `P_PRE` task. Initiating it starts a chain of events (input transfer, `P_PROC`, output transfer) that will consume resources and generate network traffic. The key insight is to regulate the admission rate based on the state of the downstream bottleneck—the UP FIFO queue. If the queue is growing, it signals that the Cloud computers are falling behind, and admitting more requests will only worsen the backlog, leading to increased `TDR`. A policy inspired by congestion control algorithms like TCP Vegas or AQM mechanisms could monitor the length of the UP queue and the rate of `P_PROC` completions. If the queue length exceeds a threshold or the completion rate drops, the admission rate should be throttled. Conversely, if the system is underutilized, the admission rate can be safely increased. This transforms admission from a static cap into a dynamic, context-aware decision that actively prevents the system from becoming overwhelmed, directly addressing the suspected weakness in TDR-blown tests.

Regarding **load balancing**, the current solver assigns each request to a remote at `P_PRE` and never changes it. This is a one-shot decision made with incomplete information, as the output length `Lout` is hidden until the end. This is a variant of the stochastic bin packing problem. The current heuristic of picking the remote with the minimum estimated completion time (ETC) is a reasonable greedy approach, but it can be improved. The **power-of-two-choices** heuristic is a well-known and effective method for this type of problem. The algorithm works by selecting two random remotes and assigning the new request to the one with the lesser total queued work (or fewer requests). This simple modification dramatically improves load balance compared to always picking the single best option, as it avoids concentrating too much load on a server that happened to be slightly better at one point in time. It requires minimal additional bookkeeping (just tracking the load on each remote) and fits perfectly within the problem's constraints. Another angle is to consider the request's `Lin` value. Since `Lin` is revealed at arrival, the scheduler could use it as a proxy for the request's total computational footprint. A more sophisticated policy might try to balance the total `Lin` weight across remotes rather than just the number of requests.

For **task prioritization**, the current solver uses a fixed, albeit complex, priority order for the local computer's tasks. The `cμ` rule from queueing theory provides a quantitative basis for this decision [[11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304414921000326), [12](https://www.politesi.polimi.it/bitstream/10589/139990/3/2018_04_Invernizzi_Ripanti.pdf)]. To apply it, we define the "holding cost" `c` as the marginal penalty for delaying a particular task. This can be calculated as the partial derivative of the score with respect to the task's completion time. For example, completing a `P_POST` early lowers the TDR for that request, so its `c` value is proportional to `w_c`. Similarly, starting a `D_PRE` batch early helps produce tokens sooner, lowering the TPOT, so its `c` value is also proportional to `w_c`. The "service rate" `μ` is the rate at which work is done by the task; for a decode batch of size `m`, it's inversely proportional to the decode time of that batch. The scheduler would then calculate `c * μ` for every ready task (e.g., the next piece of every `P_PROC` queue, every ready decode batch) and select the one with the highest value. This creates a dynamic priority system that automatically adjusts to the current bottleneck. If the Cloud computers are backed up, the `μ` for decode batches will be low, causing their priority to drop relative to `P_POST` tasks, which have a fixed `μ`. This could balance the competing goals of the scoring function. Given the complexity of calculating the `cμ` index for all possible batch sizes, a practical implementation would involve pre-calculating indices for a range of batch sizes and selecting the best available option.

## Formal Frameworks for Online Utility Maximization and Trade-off Management

When faced with competing objectives like maximizing throughput (`tp`) and satisfying Service Level Objectives (`norm_c`), formal frameworks from stochastic control and online optimization provide a structured way to make decisions. These methods, while often more complex to implement, offer provable performance guarantees and a principled way to navigate the trade-offs defined by the scoring function. For the ICPC problem, the Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty method stands out as a particularly relevant technique.

The **Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty (LDPP)** method is a method for stabilizing queueing networks while optimizing time-averaged performance objectives, which is relevant to the problem's throughput and latency targets [[44](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04291v1), [46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_plus_penalty)]. The core idea is to define a Lyapunov function, typically the sum of the squares of the queue lengths, which serves as a measure of system congestion. The "drift" is the expected change in this function over a time slot. The LDPP algorithm greedily chooses actions at each time step to minimize a bound on the expression `Drift + V * Penalty`, where `V` is a non-negative weight parameter [[46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_plus_penalty)]. In the context of the ICPC problem, we can map this formulation as follows:

- **Queues:** We can define real queues for the work waiting on the Edge computer (ready requests) and virtual queues to enforce the SLO constraints on TDR and TPOT [[46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_plus_penalty)]. The virtual queue dynamics ensure that if the queue remains stable (i.e., its length doesn't grow without bound), the time-average of the SLO violation is kept low.
- **Drift:** The drift term penalizes the growth of these queues. By minimizing drift, the scheduler implicitly works to minimize waiting times, which directly impacts TDR and TPOT.
- **Penalty:** The penalty term represents the negative of the utility we want to maximize. Here, it would be `-tokens_produced_in_this_step`.
- **Trade-off Parameter (V):** The parameter `V` acts as a dial to control the trade-off between stability (minimizing delay) and utility (maximizing throughput). A large `V` places more emphasis on minimizing the penalty (maximizing throughput), while a small `V` prioritizes minimizing the drift (reducing delay).

By framing the problem this way, the LDPP method naturally yields a policy that balances throughput and delay. At each event frame, the scheduler would evaluate the potential outcomes of different task assignments and choose the combination that results in the lowest value of `Drift + V * Penalty`. This provides a formal, mathematical justification for the current solver's "shadow price" heuristic, which attempts to compare the marginal value of throughput gained versus the loss in score from increased waiting time. The main advantage of LDPP is that it comes with a strong theoretical guarantee: it can achieve a time-average penalty within `O(1/V)` of the optimal value, while the average queue size grows as `O(V)` [[46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_plus_penalty)]. This means we can systematically tune `V` to find a policy on the desired throughput-latency Pareto front. However, the feasibility is moderate. Calculating the exact drift requires knowing future event probabilities, which are unknown. However, a "drift-plus-penalty" method can be implemented using instantaneous measurements, which is suitable for this deterministic but unknown arrival process [[44](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04291v1)]. The main challenges are the complexity of calculating the drift term and the difficulty of tuning the parameter `V` effectively.

Another relevant area is **multi-armed bandits (MAB)** and reinforcement learning (RL), which are used to tune hyperparameters online [[149](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstreams/585e925b-f76f-494a-90a4-d4f578542ee5/download)]. The current solver's static `alpha` scalar is a major limitation. An MAB approach could treat different scheduling strategies (e.g., different batch caps, different admission thresholds) as "arms." The scheduler would then use an algorithm like UCB or Thompson Sampling to explore these strategies and exploit the one that performs best on the current test case [[97](http://www.schapire.net/papers/adversarial-bandits-with-knapsacks.pdf)]. This could be used to dynamically tune the parameters of the batch policy or the admission controller. For example, the scheduler could maintain a set of possible batch size multipliers and use bandit feedback (the resulting score change) to learn the optimal multiplier for the current configuration of `w_tp`, `K`, and network latency. This is feasible as it only requires offline simulation and A/B testing to gather data on the performance of different parameter settings. The risk is that it adds another layer of complexity and may not converge quickly enough to provide benefits during the 15-second time limit, unless the tuning happens entirely offline.

Finally, **mathematical programming** techniques like Lagrangian relaxation and primal-dual algorithms can be used for offline planning or to derive structural insights [[5](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/serving/data_parallel_deployment/), [134](https://www.kunwar.page/chapter/023-batching-static-dynamic-continuous-orca)]. While the live solver must be reactive, these methods can be used to solve relaxed versions of the problem offline to generate warm-start policies or to understand the fundamental limits of performance. For example, one could formulate the problem as an integer program and relax the integrality constraints to get a lower bound on the optimal makespan. This can help validate whether the current solver is close to optimal or if there is significant room for improvement. Primal-dual online algorithms are designed to work in an online setting and could inspire a simpler, dual-based update rule for the scheduler's parameters.

## Verification plan for candidate improvements (as I executed it)

The proposals above were tested one at a time against my snapshotted solver.
The local judge exposed mechanisms and failures; official judge feedback was

The most testable candidates are prefill chunking, decode batching, and upload admission control. **Implementing chunked prefills** directly addresses the identified gap in pipelining by breaking up long `P_PROC` tasks, thereby improving Cloud computer utilization [[87](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/agrawal), [220](https://wentao.site/chunked_prefill_sarathi_summary_2/)]. Complementing this, adopting **dynamic decode batching logic** replaces the current solver's flawed EOQ-style cap with a more sophisticated, real-time optimization that balances throughput and latency [[122](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14392v1), [123](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.04013)]. To manage the flow into this optimized pipeline, a **queue-length-based admission controller** provides a principled way to throttle new requests, preventing the critical bottleneck of UP queue congestion [[80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagle%27s_algorithm), [154](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10535934)]. These three changes form a cohesive subsystem focused on resource utilization and flow control. Further refinement can be achieved by applying more formal principles to the remaining decision knobs: using the **power-of-two-choices** for robust load balancing and a **`cμ`-like index** for dynamic task prioritization, grounding the scheduler's choices in first principles rather than ad-hoc heuristics [[11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304414921000326)].

**Lyapunov optimization** offers one theoretical framework for balancing the throughput and SLO components of the score [[44](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04291v1), [46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_plus_penalty)]. While a full implementation may be too complex for the tight constraints, its core principle—a greedy minimization of a combination of delay penalties and throughput rewards—provides a powerful validation for the current solver's shadow price heuristic and inspires simpler, Lyapunov-inspired controllers. A possible extension is a closed-loop system where decisions are informed by real-time telemetry, such as computer busy times and queue lengths, allowing the scheduler to self-tune to the demands of each unique test case [[81](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.21364v1)].
